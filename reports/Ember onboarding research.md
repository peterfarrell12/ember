# Keep Ember's onboarding short and honest

Ember should not build a long quiz. The right onboarding for a free app is about two minutes after sign-in: one plain consent step, the details the calorie sum needs, a target shown as an estimate, a first food logged with guidance, and a reminder time chosen straight after that first log. The claim that more screens convert better is real but narrow: every source behind it measures trial starts or revenue at a paywall (the screen that asks for payment) in subscription apps, most of it is founder anecdote or data from companies that sell paywall tools, and the only randomised test published with full numbers (Everlove, 24,000 users, 2026) found that cutting 28 steps to 19 **raised completion from 78% to 87% with no significant change in the share who paid**. This research found no published test, in any app, showing that a longer onboarding improves retention. What does have evidence behind it for a free product is getting people to a first real use quickly, asking for sign-up and permissions as late as the product allows, setting a reminder at the moment of first success, and a streak that needs one small action a day and forgives a miss, which Ember already has. The realistic prize is modest: the median health and fitness app keeps roughly 3 to 8% of people at day 30, and Duolingo's tested first-week changes each moved retention by low single-digit percentages, not the multiples quoted in marketing. In Ireland and the UK the long-quiz playbook is also the risky one: invented social proof, false countdowns and precise weight-by-date promises breach consumer and advertising rules, and health data needs explicit consent kept separate from the terms. The decisions on sign-in, consent wording, the age limit and calorie floors are yours; each is set out at the end with a recommendation.

Research was done on 26 November 2026. Screen counts come from outside observers' recordings, mostly undated and none confirmed as an Irish or UK capture. The section on rules is a fact file, not legal advice. Where a statement about Ember's current behaviour appears, it comes from reading the code in `web/src/app/`, not from the research notes.

In the weight-loss apps the order is nearly always: goal, body details, target weight and pace, questions about obstacles and habits, a "building your plan" loading screen, a personalised projection graph, then the price. Two patterns matter more than the counts. First, **the long flows cluster where there is something to sell at the end**: 46.4% of health and fitness apps in one sample of well-known apps show a paywall during onboarding against 22% of all apps, and 14 of 20 top US apps checked put their first paywall at the end of onboarding ([Mobbin](https://mobbin.com/blog/the-long-onboarding-a-study-of-1-460-flows), [Adapty](https://adapty.io/state-of-onboarding/report/)). Second, the famous examples are not typical. That sample of 986 well-known apps averages 25 screens, while the median across one paywall vendor's whole customer base is **5 screens, and 12 for health and fitness** ([Adapty](https://adapty.io/state-of-onboarding/report/)). Teardown culture studies the giants and so overstates how long ordinary onboarding is.

The persuasion devices are equally standard. Among the top 50 US apps, 46% ask quiz questions, 41% show a social-proof screen and 34% show a "building your plan" screen ([Adapty](https://adapty.io/state-of-onboarding/report/)). Yazio has people "tap and hold" to commit to their goal, asks for a store rating before any feature has been used, and shows a spin-the-wheel discount when the first offer is closed ([ScreensDesign](https://screensdesign.com/showcase/yazio-calorie-counter-diet)). Noom's app runs a 15-minute countdown to "reserve" the plan; its web funnel in April 2026 still showed a "3,627,436 people" statistic dated October 2021, and the pace a user chose did not change the projected end date ([ScreensDesign](https://screensdesign.com/showcase/noom-weight-loss-food-tracker), [RevenueCat](https://www.revenuecat.com/blog/growth/web-to-app-onboarding-funnel.md)). The founders who have spoken on the record are candid about the mechanism. Cal AI's co-founder says adding questions that did not change the calorie estimate raised conversion, and Lose It! says it kept lengthening its survey with questions whose answers were not used until the gains stopped ([Founderpath](https://founderpath.com/resources/zach-yadegari-cal-ai-onboarding-tests-and-influencer-marketing), [RevenueCat](https://www.revenuecat.com/blog/growth/50-million-users-5-key-strategies-lessons-from-lose-it.md)). The questions are a sales pitch, not personalisation.

The same playbook has a record. **Apple removed Cal AI from the App Store in April 2026** for a payment flow that bypassed in-app purchase, a paywall that made the weekly price more prominent than the amount billed, a trial toggle that obscured renewal, and a second offer shown after people declined the first ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2026/04/21/apples-cal-ai-crackdown-signals-its-still-policing-the-app-store/)). Noom agreed a **$62 million class-action settlement in 2022** over "risk-free" trials that rolled into renewals that were hard to cancel ([Euronews](https://www.euronews.com/next/2022/02/14/noom-settlement)). Both cases concern the price screens at the end of onboarding, not the questions.

The admired apps outside calorie tracking teach different lessons from the ones usually drawn. Duolingo is the only one built like Ember, free at the core: a handful of one-tap questions, a real lesson, and only then a sign-up prompt that can be dismissed, with the profile deferred to later ([UserGuiding](https://userguiding.com/blog/duolingo-onboarding-ux)). Headspace, Calm and Blinkist are subscription businesses that put the paywall inside onboarding, and their best published results are about honesty and timing around the ask. Blinkist replaced its trial paywall with a timeline (access today, a reminder two days before the trial ends, the charge on day seven) and reported trial conversion up 23%, complaints down 55% and **notification opt-in up from 6% to 74%**, because the notification was tied to something people wanted; this is self-reported from 2020 and one developer who copied it saw a fall ([Purchasely](https://www.purchasely.com/blog/blinkist-paywall-transformation-revolutionizes-app-user-engagement), [Slopes](https://blog.curtisherbert.com/slopes-diaries-39-risky-business/)). Calm found a daily-reminder setting buried in Settings, moved the prompt to just after the first completed session, and 40% of people shown it set one ([Amplitude](https://www.amplitude.com/case-studies/calm)). Descriptions of Calm's current flow conflict with one another, so no screen count is given here. Flo, which keeps cycle tracking free, offers the nearest rationale to Ember's position: its growth lead says about 40% of its subscriptions start more than six months after install and that a test which lifts onboarding conversion can lose money later ([RevenueCat](https://www.revenuecat.com/blog/growth/mike-charniatsou-flo-sub-club-podcast-2026.md)).

## The case for long onboarding rests on paywall data and anecdote

The claim traces to a small number of sources. A 2023 RevenueCat article relayed Lose It!'s remark that trial starts rose "double digits" as its onboarding grew, with no figures, sample or test design, and the author's own caveat that most apps should still keep friction low and check long-term revenue ([RevenueCat](https://www.revenuecat.com/blog/growth/why-your-onboarding-experience-might-be-too-short)). Cal AI's founder said added questions raised an undefined "conversion rate", and in the same interview named his biggest win as something else entirely: "moving the sign in screens to the end because there is a lot of drop off there" ([Founderpath](https://founderpath.com/resources/zach-yadegari-cal-ai-onboarding-tests-and-influencer-marketing)). The one large dataset is Adapty's 2026 benchmark, from a company that sells paywall tooling and does not state its sample size.

That benchmark is worth reading closely because it shows both halves of the trade. As flows lengthen from 1 to 5 screens to 21 or more, **completion falls from 89.7% to 62.8%** and trial starts per person who opens the app fall from 4.95% to between 2.1% and 2.8%, while paid subscriptions per open rise from 1.20% to 2.33% and revenue per open from $0.36 to $1.39 ([Adapty](https://adapty.io/state-of-onboarding/report/)). Adapty itself says this is not cause and effect: long flows are mostly health and education apps "which sell differently", and people who leave early had less intent to begin with. The most telling figure is that **between 1 and 20 screens revenue per open is flat at $0.33 to $0.48**; the jump appears only at 21 screens and above, where the high-priced quiz-funnel apps sit. That is a category and price effect at least as much as a length effect.

The industry's logic is specific to its business model. In subscription apps 82% of trial starts happen on the day of install, so onboarding is the only pitch most people will ever see ([RevenueCat](https://www.revenuecat.com/state-of-subscription-apps-2025)). Apps that will not work without payment convert a median 10.7% of installs to paid within 35 days against 2.1% for apps with a free tier, yet **twelve-month retention of yearly subscribers is the same for both, 27% against 28%**; RevenueCat's own conclusion is that the choice is about conversion, not retention ([RevenueCat](https://www.revenuecat.com/state-of-subscription-apps-2026/)).

| Claim or source | What was actually measured | How strong | Does it transfer to a free app? |
|---|---|---|---|
| Lose It!, Cal AI: more questions converted better | Trial starts or an undefined "conversion rate" at a paywall; no numbers published | Anecdote | No |
| Adapty 2026: 21+ screens earn more per open | Revenue and paid subscriptions across apps; completion falls | Correlation, vendor data, sample undisclosed | No; the completion loss does |
| Everlove 2026: 28 steps against 19 | Randomised, 24,000 users; completion, trial starts, lifetime value, activation | The strongest evidence found | Partly: shorter raised completion, activation unchanged |
| Headspace 2026: a short quiz | Five variants on new trial users; course starts and days meditated; sample size not reported | Controlled test | Yes, with a warning: starts doubled, days of use did not move |
| Duolingo: sign-up moved after the first lesson | Daily active users, about +20%, later +8.2% | Company's own tests, run before 2017 | Yes in direction; size unknown |
| Calm: reminder prompt after first session | 40% set a reminder; reminder-setters retained about three times better | Vendor case study, about 2016; comparison is still between people who chose to set one and people who did not | Yes in direction |
| Mobbin: famous apps are long | Screen counts only; no outcomes | Description | No |

The experiments are more useful than the benchmarks. In the Everlove test the longer flow converted more of the people who reached the paywall (3.83% against 3.13%), but fewer reached it, so conversion among all registered users was statistically flat (3.35% against 3.11%); value per registered user leaned towards the shorter flow, weakly (p = 0.093), and the app's activation event did not move at all ([RevenueCat](https://www.revenuecat.com/blog/growth/everlove-onboarding-experiment)). "Conversion went up when we added screens" is therefore compatible with no extra customers, because the denominator shrinks. Headspace's test with Irrational Labs found that asking a few questions raised course starts by 7.6 percentage points even when the recommended course was identical, that genuinely matching the course added nothing more, and that **none of the variants changed the number of days people meditated** ([Irrational Labs](https://irrationallabs.com/case-studies/headspace-doubled-course-starts/)). Flo added one question twice and lost almost 10% of conversion each time because three-quarters of users could not answer it; rewritten so people could answer, it won by 23% ([RevenueCat](https://www.revenuecat.com/blog/growth/mike-charniatsou-flo-sub-club-podcast-2026.md)). Whether a step can be answered and is visibly used matters more than how many steps there are.

The psychology quoted in support is weaker than its confidence suggests. The "foot in the door" effect averages r = .17 in meta-analysis, depends on prosocial requests, and showed no link between effort on the first request and later compliance ([Dillard et al.](https://pure.psu.edu/en/publications/sequentialrequest-persuasive-strategies-metaanalysis-of-footinthe/)). The IKEA effect appears only when people complete something they then own ([Norton et al.](https://papers.ssrn.com/abstract=1777100)). The "labour illusion" behind plan-building screens was shown for waits in which a site was visibly doing real work on a search the person had asked for; whether an invented "analysing your answers" animation has the same effect is untested, and one vendor-reported test that removed a loading screen saw trial conversions rise 22% ([Buell and Norton](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/fpi/10.1287/mnsc.1110.1376), [Adapty](https://adapty.io/blog/how-to-fix-your-onboarding-flow/)). The effect that best explains why a generic plan feels personal is the Barnum effect, in which students rated an identical personality sketch about 4.3 out of 5 for accuracy ([Forer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barnum_effect)). Using it on purpose would break Ember's rule that nothing fake is presented as real. The two mechanisms with strong evidence are different in kind: **making a specific when-and-where plan (d = .65) and setting a goal (d = .34)** both help people act afterwards, in behaviour-change research generally, not in app onboarding ([Gollwitzer and Sheeran, via PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8149892), [Epton et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29189034/)). They justify two short steps, not thirty screens.

So the line between solid and hype falls here. Solid: long quizzes raise the share of paywall viewers who start a trial, and cost completion. Unproven: that they add paying customers overall. Unsupported: that they improve retention, or that any of this applies where there is no paywall. In a paywalled app the people who abandon a long quiz cost nothing and the finishers pay more. In a free app everyone lost before first use is simply lost, with no conversion gained in exchange. The honest gap is that **nobody has published an experiment on onboarding length in a free consumer app with retention as the outcome**, so Ember's own numbers will be the evidence.

## Sign-up placement and first use matter more than screen count

The best-supported onboarding change in consumer apps is not adding or removing questions but moving the account wall. Duolingo's growth lead attributed about a 20% rise in daily active users to moving sign-up back a few steps so that people tried a lesson first, and a further 8.2% to tuning optional and mandatory sign-up prompts; a quiet "Later" button beat a large "Discard my progress" one ([First Round Review](https://review.firstround.com/the-tenets-of-a-b-testing-from-duolingos-master-growth-hacker)). Those figures are from tests run before 2017 on a product that needs no account to work, so they show direction, not a size Ember can expect. Around 90% of Duolingo's new learners outside its under-13 flow still registered once sign-up came after value ([Duolingo](https://blog.duolingo.com/lessons-from-asia-turning-local-research-into-global-experiments)). Usability research agrees: Nielsen Norman Group says a login wall is justified only where the product is inherently personal, Android's design guidance says to show value before asking for an account, and "mandatory account creation" is a named reason people abandon health apps in a review of 18 studies ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/login-walls/), [Android](https://developer.android.com/design/ui/mobile/guides/patterns/onboarding), [Kidman et al.](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11694054/)).

Ember's sign-in-first rule therefore sits on the costly side of the evidence, and that should be said plainly. It is also defensible, since sync, the coach link, squads and the per-person AI limit all depend on an account. Where the wall is fixed, the relevant evidence is Monzo's: when an account is unavoidable, time and effort inside the wall are the lever. Its US redesign lifted sign-up conversion **from 9% to 40%** and cut the time taken from 17 minutes to 9 ([Monzo](https://monzo.com/blog/how-we-cut-time-to-sign-up-for-monzo-us-from-17-minutes-to-4-minutes)). The practical reading is to keep the sign-in screen to the fewest fields, say in one line what the account is for, and put the first useful moment immediately behind it. On sign-in method, every comparison found is published by a company selling login tools; they consistently report that a one-field emailed link or a one-tap platform sign-in completes better than email plus password, but no independent head-to-head exists and an emailed link forces a switch to the mail app on a phone ([SignupDrop](https://signupdrop.pages.dev/magic-link-vs-password)). Ember already offers both routes, which is the sensible hedge.

Once someone is in, the measured wins come from one concrete first action while motivation is highest. Duolingo's first lesson is its onboarding. Canva asked what the person wanted to make and routed them into that design, lifting activation about 10% ([Appcues](https://www.appcues.com/blog/canva-growth-process)). Headspace doubled course starts from about 31% to 63% by placing the start inside onboarding, and then saw no change in repeat use partly because returning users could not find their course on the home screen ([Irrational Labs](https://irrationallabs.com/case-studies/headspace-doubled-course-starts/)). The lesson is double: put the first log inside onboarding, and make sure the second visit lands on something obvious to do. The loss is front-loaded too: the first screen alone loses 10.2% of sessions, about five times any later screen ([Adapty](https://adapty.io/state-of-onboarding/report/)).

The "aha moment" numbers that circulate (seven friends in ten days, 2,000 messages) are correlations chosen as round numbers, and the analysts who examined them say the right use is to pick the lowest threshold most retained users reach, then nudge people towards it and see whether retention follows ([Mode](https://mode.com/blog/facebook-aha-moment-simpler-than-you-think/)). MyFitnessPal's equivalent, that people who log on four days of their first week are "seven times" more likely to make progress, comes from unpublished internal data released alongside a contest requiring four logged days ([MyFitnessPal](https://news.myfitnesspal.com/the-four-day-habit-behind-one-of-myfitnesspals-biggest-success-predictors/)). It is a reasonable starting guess for Ember's own activation measure and nothing more.

Permissions follow the same rule of value first. Median opt-in to notifications is **about 49% on iPhone**, with the best tenth of apps reaching about 74%; Android fell from 71% to about 60% in a year after it began asking explicitly ([Airship](https://growth.airship.com/rs/313-QPJ-195/images/Airship-2025-Push-Notification-Benchmarks-EN.pdf?version=0)). The gap between median and top is the prize for good timing. Apple's guidance says to wait "until people actually use an app feature that requires access" and Android's says to let people get familiar with the app first ([Apple](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/privacy), [Android](https://developer.android.com/develop/ui/views/notifications/notification-permission)). The iPhone prompt can be shown only once, so a refusal is close to permanent ([OneSignal](https://documentation.onesignal.com/docs/en/prompt-for-push-permissions.md)). The cases that worked (Blinkist, Calm) tied the ask to a promise the person had just opted into. No public data exists on how often people grant camera, microphone or Apple Health access, so for those the only guide is Apple's own: ask at the first tap of scan, talk or connect, with a sentence saying exactly what it is for.

## Most people leave within weeks, and small first-week choices help a little

The baseline is harsh. Vendor benchmarks for health and fitness put retention at roughly **20 to 28% on day 1, 7 to 18% on day 7 and 3 to 8% on day 30**; the low end counts every install and the high end counts only apps with an active messaging programme, so the figures should not be averaged ([GetStream, relaying AppsFlyer and OneSignal](https://getstream.io/blog/app-retention-guide/)). Peer-reviewed work agrees: a median of 70% of users of lifestyle apps stop within 100 days, with diet apps at 86%, and in two weight-loss trials fewer than half of participants were still tracking after week 10 ([Kidman et al.](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11694054/), [Turner-McGrievy et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31155473/)). The steepest loss is in the first days, so anything meant to help has to act inside the first week.

Three findings shape what that week should ask of people. First, a low bar counts. In an eight-week study frequency and consistency of logging predicted weight loss while completeness of the logs did not, and the best marker in the trial data was simply days with two or more eating occasions logged ([Payne et al.](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9159560), [Turner-McGrievy et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31155473/)). All "loggers lose more" figures come from the minority who kept logging (one cohort was about 1.2% of new sign-ups), so they describe survivors, not the effect of logging; the honest claim is that people who log consistently tend to do better ([News-Medical](https://www.news-medical.net/news/20260825/Consistent-meal-logging-drives-major-weight-loss-in-app-users.aspx)). Second, habits take months: the median is about 66 days with a range from 18 to over 250, a missed day does no measurable harm, and gym habits take months where simple ones take weeks ([UCL](https://www.ucl.ac.uk/news/2009/aug/how-long-does-it-take-form-habit), [Buyalskaya et al.](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10151500)). No first week forms a habit, and Ember should never say it does. Third, effort is the most consistently reported reason people stop food logging, alongside distrust of the numbers and guilt after a miss ([Kidman et al.](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11694054/)). Faster logging is plausible as a fix but unproven: the one randomised comparison, from 2022 and before current AI recognition, found photo logging harder and less sustained than text although people predicted the opposite ([Silverman et al.](https://www.journals.uchicago.edu/doi/10.1086/720444)). Offer every method and watch which one each person uses; do not ask people to predict.

Duolingo is the only company that publishes tested results for first-week devices, and the sizes are sobering. Letting one lesson extend the streak, instead of requiring a full daily goal, raised day-14 retention by 3.3%; nearly 40% of people who were active two days running with no streak had picked the hardest daily goal, so ambitious self-set goals were blocking streaks ([Duolingo](https://blog.duolingo.com/improving-the-streak/)). Streak protection over a weekend raised day-7 retention by 2.1% and day-14 by 4%; new milestone animations raised day-7 retention of new learners by 1.7% ([First Round Review](https://review.firstround.com/the-tenets-of-a-b-testing-from-duolingos-master-growth-hacker), [Duolingo](https://blog.duolingo.com/how-duolingo-streak-builds-habit/)). The same company reports that people on a 7-day streak are 2.4 times more likely to return the next day, which is a correlation. **The gap between the multiples and the tested lifts is the size of the selection effect**, and it is the gap between hype and evidence in this whole field.

The academic work points the same way as Duolingo's tests. Experiments show a visibly broken streak reduces later engagement regardless of what the person actually did, and that a way to repair the streak removes most of the harm ([Silverman and Barasch, summary](https://www.colorado.edu/business/faculty-research/2023/04/19/or-track-how-broken-streaks-affect-consumer-decisions)). Gamification raises physical activity in randomised trials (g = 0.42), but only g = 0.23 against a non-gamified active control and g = 0.15 some 14 weeks after the mechanic is removed ([Mazeas et al.](https://www.jmir.org/2022/1/e26779)). Small-group competition is the one social mechanic whose effect outlasted the intervention in a trial ([Patel et al.](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6735420)). And reminders cut both ways: "annoying notifications" is a named reason for quitting, and a 2025 UCL and Loughborough study of posts about fitness and calorie apps found shame and giving up after missed targets and lost streaks ([Kidman et al.](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11694054/), [UCL](https://www.ucl.ac.uk/news/2025/oct/emotional-strain-fitness-and-calorie-counting-apps-revealed)).

Ember already has the evidence-backed form of most of this. In the code, a day counts towards the streak if anything at all was logged, a freeze covers a miss, a break holds the streak, and a new person starts with one freeze. What the first week lacks is the two things that have measured support and are not yet in the flow: a guided first log before the person leaves the first session, and a reminder time chosen at that moment.

## Irish and UK rules strike out most of the persuasion playbook

### The app stores set the frame for sign-in, permissions and AI

Apple's rule 5.1.1(v) reads: "If your app doesn't include significant account-based features, let people use it without a login", and requires account deletion inside the app wherever accounts exist ([Apple](https://developer.apple.com/app-store/review/guidelines/)). Ember's sign-in wall is defensible only if review accepts that sync, coaching, squads and the AI limit are significant account features, so those should be named in the notes for review; a rejection on this ground is a real risk because search, scanning and manual logging could work without an account. Apple also requires apps to "clearly disclose where personal data will be shared with third parties, including with third-party AI, and obtain explicit permission before doing so", wording added in November 2025; sending a meal photo or voice transcript to Anthropic falls under it ([Apple](https://developer.apple.com/app-store/review/guidelines/)). Its design guidance says permission requests belong in onboarding only if the app cannot function without them, and that a lead-in screen before a system prompt should have a single button titled "Continue" or "Next", not "Allow" ([Apple](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/privacy)). Asking for sex, age, height, weight and goal is "directly relevant to the core functionality" of a calorie target; questions the product does not use are harder to defend under Apple's data-minimisation rule. Adding Google or Facebook sign-in later would trigger Apple's requirement to offer an equivalent privacy-preserving login alongside it.

Google Play requires a route to delete the account and its data both inside the app and on a public web page, a health apps declaration for every app, a prominent in-app disclosure and consent before health data is collected (a privacy policy alone does not count), and a store description stating the app is "not a medical device and does not diagnose, treat, cure, or prevent any medical condition" ([Google Play user data](https://support.google.com/googleplay/android-developer/answer/10144311?hl=en), [Google Play health](https://support.google.com/googleplay/android-developer/answer/16679511?hl=en)). If an adults-only audience is declared, the age question must be neutral, with no pre-set date that hints at the cut-off ([Google Play](https://support.google.com/googleplay/android-developer/answer/9867159?hl=en)).

### Health data needs its own explicit consent, and age is two different numbers

Ember's profile and diary should be treated as special category health data. The UK regulator's own examples say a device that "records BMI scores" is handling health data, and the EU's top court held in 2024 that data is health data where health status can be worked out "by means of an intellectual operation involving comparison or deduction" ([ICO](https://ico.org.uk/for-organisations/uk-gdpr-guidance-and-resources/lawful-basis/special-category-data/what-is-special-category-data/), [CJEU](https://curia.europa.eu/jcms/upload/docs/application/pdf/2024-10/cp240159en.pdf)). No regulator decision says in terms that a calorie diary is always health data; it is the safe assumption. Explicit consent "must be confirmed by the person in a clear statement", should be "separate from any other consents you're seeking", must not be "bundled up with other terms and conditions", must offer "granular consent options for different types of processing" and must name any third party relying on it ([ICO conditions](https://ico.org.uk/for-organisations/uk-gdpr-guidance-and-resources/lawful-basis/special-category-data/what-are-the-conditions-for-processing/), [ICO consent](https://ico.org.uk/for-organisations/uk-gdpr-guidance-and-resources/lawful-basis/consent/what-is-valid-consent/)). Consent to the core calculation can be a condition of using the app because targets cannot be worked out without it; AI features, coach sharing, wearables and marketing are not necessary for manual logging, so each needs its own opt-in.

Today Ember asks for all of this in one tick on the sign-in form: being 18 or over, the Terms, the Privacy policy, storing health information and AI processing by Anthropic in the United States (`web/src/app/08y-account.js`). That is honest and specific, but it bundles what the guidance says to separate. Splitting it is the single most useful onboarding change on the compliance side, and the wording is yours to settle with whoever advises you.

On age, Ireland's digital age of consent is 16 and the UK's is 13, and an adults-only rule does not by itself take a service outside the UK Children's Code, which applies if children are more likely than not to use it ([DPC](https://www.dataprotection.ie/en/dpc-guidance/blogs/the-children-fundamentals), [ICO](https://ico.org.uk/for-organisations/uk-gdpr-guidance-and-resources/childrens-information/childrens-code-guidance-and-resources/likely-to-be-accessed-by-children/)). MyFitnessPal requires users to be 18, and third-party reports of Noom's terms say the same ([MyFitnessPal](https://www.myfitnesspal.com/terms-of-service)). Ember's tick says 18 or over, while the targets form accepts any age from 14 (`web/src/app/04-flows.js`); the two should agree. A data protection impact assessment is needed before launch on both regulators' lists, and transfers to a US AI provider rest on contract clauses that Anthropic's data processing addendum already contains, with the EU-US framework under appeal ([ICO](https://ico.org.uk/for-organisations/uk-gdpr-guidance-and-resources/accountability-and-governance/data-protection-impact-assessments-dpias/examples-of-processing-likely-to-result-in-high-risk), [DPC](https://www.dataprotection.ie/en/organisations/know-your-obligations/data-protection-impact-assessments), [Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/legal/data-processing-addendum), [IAPP](https://iapp.org/news/a/european-general-court-dismisses-latombe-challenge-upholds-eu-us-data-privacy-framework)).

### Fake urgency and fake reviews are banned outright; weight-by-date promises are the grey area

In the UK the Digital Markets, Competition and Consumers Act has banned 32 practices in all circumstances since 6 April 2025, including falsely stating an offer is time-limited (the regulator's example is a countdown clock) and submitting or commissioning fake reviews; the Competition and Markets Authority now fines directly and had fined the AA £4.2 million and others by mid-2026 ([CMA](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/unfair-commercial-practices-cma207/unfair-commercial-practices), [Mayer Brown](https://www.mayerbrown.com/en/insights/publications/2026/08/were-just-getting-started-the-cmas-new-consumer-enforcement-powers-in-action)). Ireland's Consumer Rights Act 2022 prohibits fake reviews and requires reasonable steps to verify any reviews shown ([CCPC](https://www.ccpc.ie/news-and-media/news/article/2022/11/28/ccpc-welcomes-new-rights-for-consumers)). A loading animation is not itself unlawful; it becomes a misleading practice when it claims analysis that is not happening. The EU's proposed Digital Fairness Act, published on 11 November 2026 and not yet law, would go further: per a law-firm summary it would ban framing the decline option as the wrong choice and require an easy way to switch off "features encouraging engagement at regular times, including through penalties", which describes a strict streak ([Cuatrecasas](https://www.cuatrecasas.com/en/latam/intellectual-property/art/european-commission-digital-fairness-act)). Ember's break and freeze, plus a setting to turn streaks and reminders off, would anticipate it.

The advertising codes bite on the projection screen that every long flow ends with. The UK code says "Marketing communications must not contain claims that people can lose precise amounts of weight within a stated period", bars weight-reduction marketing aimed at under-18s or at anyone who would end below BMI 18.5, and treats more than 2 lb (just under 1 kg) a week as incompatible with good practice; Ireland's code has a matching 1 kg limit ([CAP Code](https://www.asa.org.uk/type/non_broadcast/code_section/13.html), [ASAI](https://asai.ie/asai-code/slimming)). Whether a "you will reach 78 kg by 14 March" screen inside an app counts as advertising is unsettled and no ruling on a calorie app's own projection was found; the same sentence in a store screenshot, website or social post plainly is. Ember's pace options stop at 0.75 kg a week, which is inside the limit. Its targets card and goal-weight feature do show a forecast date. The lower-risk form is a rate labelled as an estimate with "results vary", never a date in any marketing image, and no projection at all for a goal below BMI 18.5. CAP runs a free copy-advice service that could settle the in-app question.

### Eating-disorder safeguards are convention, not law, and are worth adopting anyway

Studies associate calorie-tracking apps with eating-disorder symptoms: in one sample of 105 people with a diagnosis, about 75% had used MyFitnessPal and 73% of those said it contributed ([Levinson et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28843591/)). Experiments have not shown that tracking causes symptoms in low-risk users ([PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9109125)). The charity Beat has asked for screening when people join and criticised an NHS app for warning low-BMI users without blocking them ([Tyla](https://tyla.com/health/calorie-counting-apps-impact-eating-disorders-354995-20230403), [Beat](https://beateatingdisorders.org.uk/news/beat-campaigners-celebrate-improvement-in-nhs-app)). NICE places diets of 800 to 1,200 kcal a day in specialist services, and MyFitnessPal is reported to refuse targets below 1,200 kcal for women and 1,500 for men ([NICE](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/NG246/chapter/physical-activity-and-diet), [Tyla](https://tyla.com/health/calorie-counting-apps-impact-eating-disorders-354995-20230403)). No Apple, Google, UK or Irish rule specific to calorie apps was found.

Ember's automatic targets already hold at 1,200 and 1,500 kcal and say so. Two gaps are visible in the code: a target typed by hand is accepted down to 1,000 kcal, and the goal-weight sheet accepts any figure from 30 kg with no check against BMI 18.5 that I could find. The safeguards the evidence supports are a block (offering maintain or gain instead) on weight-loss goals below BMI 18.5, the same floor for typed targets, signposting to Beat in the UK and Bodywhys in Ireland plus "speak to your GP", and game rewards that pay for logging, never for eating less. Finally, staying on the wellness side of the medical-device line depends on wording: "general fitness, general health and general wellbeing" is not a medical purpose, but claims to treat or manage a condition are, and a disclaimer does not cure a medical claim made elsewhere ([MHRA](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/6abf6c8c943e890cb55e5e52/Medical_device_stand-alone_software_including_apps__including_IVDMDs.pdf)). Marketing email needs its own unticked opt-in in both countries and cannot be a condition of the account ([ICO](https://ico.org.uk/for-organisations/direct-marketing-and-privacy-and-electronic-communications/guidance-on-direct-marketing-using-electronic-mail/how-do-we-comply-with-the-pecr-electronic-mail-marketing-rules/), [DPC](https://www.dataprotection.ie/en/dpc-guidance/faq-consent-electronic-direct-marketing)). The UK Equality Act duty to make reasonable adjustments applies whatever the size of the business, so the sign-up flow should meet WCAG 2.2 AA: labelled fields, a typed alternative to any slider, paste allowed in the password field ([Scope](https://business.scope.org.uk/uk-law-and-digital-accessibility-what-it-means-for-your-organisation/), [W3C](https://www.w3.org/WAI/standards-guidelines/wcag/new-in-22/)).

## Recommended flow: about two minutes, ending with a logged food

Ember's current first run is closer to right than the industry's. After sign-in, one sheet titled "Set your daily targets" asks sex, age, height and weight, with activity, goal and pace already set to defaults, shows the targets updating live as the details go in, and ends on "You are set" with a prompt to log a first food. That live card is the honest version of a plan-building screen: real arithmetic, shown at once, editable. The recommendation is to keep it and add three things around it: a consent step of its own before it, a guided first log after it, and a reminder choice after that log. Nothing else should be added before first use. The two-minute figure is my estimate from the number of fields and should be checked with a stopwatch on real people.

| Step | What the person sees | Why it is there | How strong the support is |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Sign-in (exists) | Email and password, or an emailed link. One line on what the account is for: your diary saved and on every device, coach link, squads. | The wall is your decision; when a wall is fixed, effort inside it is the lever (Monzo). Naming account features also supports Apple review. | Direction solid, size unknown |
| 2. Health information consent (new, own screen) | A short statement of what Ember stores and why, with the button itself as the statement. Age confirmed here by a neutral question. Link to the full notice. | ICO, Apple and Google all point to a separate, explicit, unbundled consent before the first health question. | Regulatory guidance; wording is yours |
| 3. Your goal and details (exists) | Lose, maintain or gain; sex, age, height, weight, activity; pace if not maintaining. One line under each saying why it is asked. | Every answer changes the target, so every question is used. One answered question also raises the next action (Headspace). | Needed for the product to work |
| 4. Your targets (exists) | Calories and macros, marked as an estimate from a formula, with the sum shown ("you burn about X; this target is Y below that, about 0.5 kg a week"). Editable. No loading delay, no date promise. | Reflects the answers back as something the person shaped; avoids the advertising rule on precise loss by a date. | Mechanism plausible; honesty is your own rule |
| 5. Log one thing now (new) | "What have you eaten today?" with search, scan, say it or photo. One item is enough. Camera or microphone is requested only when that option is tapped; the first AI use asks permission naming Anthropic. | Best-supported step in the whole flow: first real use in the first session (Duolingo, Canva, Headspace). Permissions in context per Apple and Android. | Several company tests; none in a calorie app |
| 6. Day one, and a reminder (partly new) | The streak lights (exists). Then, in the phone app only: "When should Ember nudge you if nothing is logged?" with a time picker, a "Continue" button into the system prompt, and a plain way to skip. | Calm and Blinkist: ask at the moment of first success, tied to a promise the person chose. A when-and-where plan is the best-evidenced behaviour step (d = .65 in general research). | Company cases plus strong general research |
| 7. Today screen | The logged item visible, the day's remaining calories, and one quiet next step. | Headspace's lesson: a first action only helps if the second visit makes the same action obvious. | One controlled test |

Several things belong after the first session, each asked at the moment it becomes useful. Goal weight fits the first weigh-in. Training set-up fits the first visit to the Train tab. Wearables and Apple Health fit the moment someone taps connect. The coach link, squads, the meal plan and the weekly review fit the end of the first week, when there is a week to review; the evidence for social features is correlational apart from small-group competition. Diary import from another app is the exception worth a quiet link on the first-log step, since for someone switching it is the fastest route to a full diary; that is my suggestion, not a tested finding. A marketing-email opt-in belongs in settings or a later prompt, unticked. An optional "How did you hear about Ember?" asked on day two or three, never as a gate, is the tracker-free way to learn where people come from. And several things should not be built at all: a multi-screen quiz of questions the product does not use, a timed "analysing" screen, testimonials or user counts before real ones exist, countdowns, a second offer after a refusal, and any button that makes declining sound like the wrong choice.

### What to test first, given Ember's size

A/B testing in the Duolingo sense is out of reach at launch. Its growth lead put the bar at about 100,000 daily users to detect small effects and advised new apps to look only for changes worth 20 to 30% ([First Round Review](https://review.firstround.com/the-tenets-of-a-b-testing-from-duolingos-master-growth-hacker)). Ember should therefore change one large thing at a time and compare cohorts by sign-up week, keeping in mind that fitness has the strongest seasonal swing of any category, so January sign-ups should be compared only with January sign-ups. The order follows the Everlove authors' own conclusion that length is not the first thing to decide: find where the funnel loses most people first ([RevenueCat](https://www.revenuecat.com/blog/growth/everlove-onboarding-experiment)). Start by measuring the current flow for a few weeks untouched. Then add the guided first log and watch the share who log in their first session, which is the change with the most support and the largest plausible effect. Then add the reminder prompt in the phone app and watch opt-in against the 49% iPhone median and day-7 return. Wording comes after that: Duolingo doubled take-up of a step by renaming "take a placement test" to "check your English level" ([Duolingo](https://blog.duolingo.com/lessons-from-asia-turning-local-research-into-global-experiments)). The largest known lever, letting people set targets and log one food before creating the account, stays available as a later test if you choose to revisit the sign-in decision; it interacts with consent and with Apple's login rule, so it would need advice first.

### What to measure without third-party trackers

Everything needed can be counted from rows Ember already stores in its own database, as totals, with no outside software and nothing new on the device. The funnel is accounts created, consent given, targets saved, first food logged, and reminder switched on, with the time from account creation to first log beside it. The activation measure to start with is the share of new people who log on at least four of their first seven days, treated as a guess to be checked against who is still logging in week four, following the rule of picking the lowest bar most retained people clear. Retention is the share with a logged day on exactly day 1, day 7 and day 30 after sign-up, by sign-up week. The benchmarks above (roughly 20 to 28%, 7 to 18% and 3 to 8%) count everyone who installed, while Ember will be counting people who got as far as an account, so Ember's figures will look better than they are by comparison. To see which onboarding step loses people, the app would need to save a small first-party record of the furthest step each account reached; that is the only new data this plan calls for, and the privacy notice should say that Ember counts its own usage in aggregate. People who reach the sign-in screen and leave are visible only as page requests in your own server logs.

### Decisions that are yours

| Decision | Recommendation | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Sign-in before use | Keep for launch; measure the drop at the sign-in screen; revisit with a "try first" test only if the numbers warrant it | The evidence says the wall costs users (Duolingo, Cal AI, the abandonment review) but not how many; Apple's rule 5.1.1(v) is a live risk either way |
| Sign-in methods | Keep email and password plus emailed link; do not add Google or Facebook yet | Adding a social login triggers Apple's equivalent-login requirement; evidence on methods is vendor-only |
| Consent wording and structure | Split the single tick into a health-information consent on its own screen, a separate opt-in at first AI use naming Anthropic and the United States, and a separate unticked marketing opt-in | ICO guidance on explicit consent; Apple 5.1.2(i); Google's prominent disclosure rule |
| Age limit | 18 and over, asked neutrally, with the targets form brought into line (it accepts 14 today) | Matches MyFitnessPal and Noom, removes the Ireland 16 and UK 13 split, fits the advertising rule on under-18s |
| Calorie floors and goal limits | Apply the 1,200 and 1,500 kcal floors to typed targets too; block weight-loss goals below BMI 18.5; have a dietitian sign off the numbers | NICE, the advertising code and market convention; Beat's criticism of warnings without blocks |
| Eating-disorder question | Offer an optional, respectful question with a route that signposts help; decide placement with advice | Beat asked for screening; the answer is itself health data needing consent |
| Forecast dates | Show a weekly rate marked as an estimate; keep specific dates out of every store image and advert; ask CAP's copy-advice service about the in-app forecast | CAP Code 13.9; in-app status unsettled |
| Streak and reminder switches | Add a setting to turn each off | Anticipates the proposed EU Digital Fairness Act; answers the guilt findings |
| First-party usage counts | Approve counting from Ember's own database and one line in the privacy notice | Needed to answer every open question in this report without trackers |

Four points need a specialist before launch and were not settled by this research: whether in-app projections fall under the advertising codes, whether squads bring Ember under the UK Online Safety Act ([Ofcom](https://www.ofcom.org.uk/online-safety/illegal-and-harmful-content/illegal-content-duties-under-the-online-safety-act)), whether Anthropic is certified under the EU-US and UK data frameworks, and whether Apple expects the developer account to be held by a company (its rule 5.1.1(ix) says apps needing sensitive information "should be submitted by a legal entity").

## Conclusion

The thing worth copying from the best apps is not their length but where they finish: Duolingo's onboarding ends with a lesson done, Fitbod's with a workout built, Calm's best step came after a session completed. Ember's should end with a food in the diary and a reminder set, and everything before that point should be something the calorie sum or the law requires. Being free turns the industry's trade-off on its head. The long quiz exists to make a price feel earned, and Ember has no price, so the devices that carry legal and trust risk elsewhere would buy it nothing, and plain honesty costs it nothing.

Two implications follow. If a paid tier arrives, the evidence says to put the persuasion immediately before that paywall, at the moment someone wants the paid feature, and to show the trial as an honest timeline with a reminder, since both the UK's subscription rules due in 2027 and Apple's action against Cal AI point the same way. And because nobody has published what onboarding length does to retention in a free app, the few first-party counts described above are not housekeeping. They are the only way anyone, including Ember's competitors, will find out.
