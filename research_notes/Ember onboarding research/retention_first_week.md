# Retention in health and fitness apps, and which onboarding and first-week choices are shown to improve it (2025 to 2026)

Provenance note for the report writer. Each finding is tagged: **[opened]** means I read the page or paper itself; **[snippet]** means the figure came from a search-result summary of that page and was not checked against the full text. Evidence type is tagged **RCT / experiment**, **quasi-experimental**, **observational**, **company claim** or **vendor benchmark**. Business of Apps, Adjust's blog, UCL's news page, MyFitnessPal's blog and two JMIR pages refused or returned empty on fetch, so their figures are snippet-level. Research compiled 26 November 2026.

## 1. Retention benchmarks for health, fitness and calorie-tracking apps (day 1, 7, 30 and longer), by source

### Takeaway
Published day-30 retention for health and fitness ranges from about 3% (AppsFlyer) to about 8% (Adjust, OneSignal); the spread is mostly method and sample, not real difference, so the figures must not be averaged. A reasonable reading is that a median health and fitness app keeps roughly 20 to 28% of installers on day 1, 7 to 18% on day 7 and 3 to 8% on day 30, and the best apps hold two to four times the median at day 30.

### Inferences
- The attribution vendors (AppsFlyer, Adjust) count every install, including paid-ad installs that never open the app twice; OneSignal and Mixpanel only see apps that have installed an engagement or analytics SDK. That alone explains most of the 3% versus 8% gap at day 30. For a product target, the AppsFlyer line (about 19 / 9 / 3) is the conservative floor and the OneSignal line (28 / 18 / 8.5) is closer to what an app with reminders and a real engagement loop should expect.
- "Best versus median" at day 30 looks like roughly 15 to 30% against 3 to 8%, on the few named-app figures available. All of those named-app figures are third-party estimates.
- Because fitness has the strongest seasonality, cohorts acquired in January will look worse at day 30 than cohorts acquired in, say, March; benchmarks and internal cohorts should be compared like for like by install month.
- Paid retention is a different quantity from usage retention: a year-one annual subscriber is "retained" in RevenueCat's sense whether or not they open the app.

### Gaps
- I could not open AppsFlyer's, Adjust's or Business of Apps' own pages (403, 429 or a listing page), so no primary table with sample sizes, app counts or an explicit classic-versus-rolling definition was verified. Every category figure above is second-hand.
- No health and fitness specific figures were retrieved from RevenueCat (2025 or 2026), Mixpanel, Amplitude, Statista or data.ai. No day-60, day-90 or 12-month usage retention benchmark from a primary source was found for the category.
- No benchmark specific to calorie or diet tracking apps (as distinct from all Health & Fitness) was found.
- No Irish or UK specific retention benchmark was found.

## 2. Peer-reviewed evidence on attrition and adherence in diet-tracking and fitness apps, and what early logging predicts

### Takeaway
Attrition is the norm: a median of 70% of users of lifestyle apps stop within 100 days and tracking frequency in diet apps falls to under half the sample by week 10. More frequent and more consistent logging is strongly associated with weight loss in every dataset, but all of that evidence is observational and drawn from the small minority who kept logging, so it cannot show that making people log more causes better outcomes.

### Cited Findings

How fast people leave:

- **Kidman et al. 2024, JMIR, scoping review:** 18 studies, 525,824 participants, published 2014 to 2022. "A median of 70% of users discontinued use within the first 100 days", with a curvilinear pattern (steep early loss, then flattening). By domain: physical activity 54 to 75% abandonment, diet 86%, mental health 89 to 92%, alcohol 95 to 97%, smoking 40% (time points vary between studies). Two included studies found half of users gone within the first days; one found half took about 240 days. Review of observational studies. [opened] — [Kidman et al. 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11694054/)
- **Meyerowitz-Katz et al. 2020, JMIR, systematic review and meta-analysis:** pooled dropout in mHealth interventions for chronic disease 43% (95% CI 29 to 57) across 17 studies (9 RCTs, 8 observational); observational studies 49%, RCTs 40%. Heterogeneity was very high (I² above 99% in the preprint). An earlier preprint gave 47% overall; the published 43% is the figure to cite. [snippet] — [Meyerowitz-Katz et al. 2020](https://www.jmir.org/2020/9/e20283)
- **Pratap et al. 2020, npj Digital Medicine:** eight remote app-based health studies, 109,914 participants, 2014 to 2019. Median retention across studies 5.5 days (study medians 2 to 26 days). Associated with longer retention: clinician referral (+40 days median), compensation (+22 days, one study), having the condition being studied (+7 days), older age (+4 days). Observational survival analysis. [snippet] — [Pratap et al. 2020](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7026051)
- **Helander et al. 2014, JMIR:** The Eatery (photo food journal), 189,770 people who downloaded and used the app at least once, October 2011 to April 2012. Only 2.58% (4,895) became "active" (at least 10 pictures and at least one week of use). Active users were more likely to already eat healthily. Observational. [snippet] — [Helander et al. 2014](https://www.jmir.org/2014/4/e109/)
- **Eysenbach 2005, JMIR, "The Law of Attrition":** proposes that in any eHealth trial a substantial share of users drop out or stop using the application; separates dropout attrition (lost to follow-up) from nonusage attrition (stopped using) and argues attrition curves are under-reported. Viewpoint paper, not data. [snippet] — [Eysenbach 2005](https://www.jmir.org/2005/1/e11)
- **Turner-McGrievy et al. 2019, J Acad Nutr Diet:** secondary analysis of two 6-month randomised weight-loss trials (91 completers). Tracking fell steadily; by every adherence definition examined, fewer than half the sample was still tracking after week 10. The best adherence marker was the number of days with at least two eating occasions tracked (R² = 0.27 for 6-month weight loss). The authors say the fast early fall points to a window for early intervention. [snippet] — [Turner-McGrievy et al. 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31155473/)
- **JMIR mHealth 2026 scoping review of calorie-counting apps:** in included trials MyFitnessPal use fell from 5.4 to 1.4 days per week between weeks 4 and 12; Lose It! use fell to 4 days per week by week 12. [snippet; page returned empty on fetch] — [JMIR mHealth 2026;e64139](https://mhealth.jmir.org/2026/1/e64139/PDF)
- **Payne et al. 2021, Obesity Science & Practice:** single-arm 8-week study, 90 employees with overweight or obesity using FatSecret. Mean logging on 50.1% of days (28.1 of 56); completers 70.2%, partial completers 25%. Frequency of logging (p = 0.013) and consistency, defined as weeks with at least three days logged (p = 0.009, about 0.23% more weight loss per consistent week), were associated with weight loss; completeness of logs was not (p = 0.062). No analysis of early logging as a predictor. Uncontrolled. [opened] — [Payne et al. 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9159560)

Large app datasets (all observational, all survivor samples):

- **Noom, Chin et al. 2016, Scientific Reports:** 35,921 Noom Coach users who installed between 10 October 2012 and 9 April 2014 and "logged in and recorded their data two or more times a month for 6 consecutive months". 77.9% reported a decrease in body weight; 22.7% lost more than 10%. Median use 267 days. Dinner input frequency was the strongest predictor of success (OR 10.69, 95% CI 6.20 to 19.53); weight input frequency OR 3.0 (2.21 to 4.08); exercise input frequency OR 2.49 (1.96 to 3.17). Frequent weigh-ins lowered the odds of yo-yo regain (OR 0.59, 0.39 to 0.89). The paper does not say how many users failed the inclusion test, so the share of all installers this represents is unknown; Table 1 totals 35,831, not 35,921. No control group. [opened] — [Chin et al. 2016](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5098151/)
- **Noom, Carey et al. 2021, JMIR mHealth:** 11,252 Noom Healthy Weight users at 9 to 16 weeks: 23.05% stable weight, 57.23% moderate loss, 19.71% high loss; weight loss was associated with articles read, meals logged, steps, coach messages, exercise logged, weigh-ins and days with at least one meal logged per week. Observational. [snippet] — [Carey et al. 2021](https://mhealth.jmir.org/2021/11/e30622)
- **MyNetDiary, Oreshko and Heikkinen 2026, JMIR:** retrospective cohort of 5,132 users, 6-month outcomes. Logging 6 to 7 days a week: 11.33% mean weight loss; fewer than 2 days a week: 6.84%; 2 to 3 days a week: 7.35%. Each extra tracking day per week: adjusted OR 1.35 (95% CI 1.31 to 1.40) for at least 5% loss. Longest streak over 90 days: 11.55% mean loss and 81.2% reaching 5%; longest streak of a week or less: 4.21% and 42.3%. The cohort was an engaged subset, about 1.2% of new sign-ups. Authors are the app's CEO and staff; GLP-1 use was not measured. [opened, via news report; JMIR page returned empty] — [News-Medical report](https://www.news-medical.net/news/20260825/Consistent-meal-logging-drives-major-weight-loss-in-app-users.aspx); [JMIR 2026;e98144](https://www.jmir.org/2026/1/e98144)
- **Lose It!, JMIR mHealth 2018:** 7,007 "regular" users, defined as logging food at least 8 times in each half of each month; the study's question was weekday versus weekend calorie consistency, not early logging. [snippet] — [JMIR mHealth 2018;e83](https://mhealth.jmir.org/2018/4/e83)

What early use predicts:

- **Lose It! trial secondary analysis (Farage et al. 2021):** using app data from the first 8 weeks, the number of self-weigh-ins was the significant predictor of 4-month weight loss; prediction of 12-month loss was weak (R² about 5%). [snippet; I did not open the paper and the exact link was not confirmed, the search surfaced it alongside [this record](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8319781)]
- **Randomised trial using a commercial app, J Behav Med 2020:** consistent trackers lost more weight than inconsistent trackers at 1 month (a within-trial observational comparison). [snippet] — [PubMed 31396820](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31396820/)
- **MyFitnessPal academic analysis:** first-week usage level and number of friends were important factors for continued engagement (reported in an arXiv paper citing earlier MyFitnessPal retention work). [snippet] — [arXiv 1510.04049](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1510.04049.pdf)
- **MyFitnessPal weight-goal analysis:** among users who set a weight goal, more calories reported per day in the first 7 days was associated with a lower likelihood of meeting the goal. [snippet] — [PMC7197296](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7197296)
- **Raber et al. 2021 systematic review (59 studies):** both lower-intensity and higher-intensity dietary self-monitoring may support weight loss, but inconsistent adherence measures limit conclusions. [snippet] — [Raber et al. 2021](https://dx.doi.org/10.1017/S136898002100358X)
- **Adherence definitions:** a 2018 review found no consensus on how to define adherence to app-based dietary self-monitoring. [snippet] — [PubMed 30115555](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30115555/)

### Inferences
- Every "loggers lose more" figure comes from a sample conditioned on continued use (Noom: logged twice a month for six months; MyNetDiary: about 1.2% of sign-ups; Lose It!: at least 8 logs per half month). They describe the survivors, not the effect of logging. The honest claim an app can make is "people who log consistently tend to do better", not "logging N days will make you lose X".
- The consistent finding across Payne, Turner-McGrievy and MyNetDiary is that a low bar counts: three days a week, or two eating occasions a day, separates the groups about as well as complete logs do. Completeness did not predict outcome in Payne et al. That supports a first-week target of "log something on most days" rather than "log everything".
- Noom's finding that dinner logging was the strongest predictor is best read as a marker of people who are still logging at the end of the day, when most people have stopped.
- Because the steepest loss is in the first days to weeks (Kidman, Pratap, Helander), any intervention aimed at retention has to act inside the first week; by week 10 half the audience has already gone.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed study was found that reports classic day-1, day-7 and day-30 retention for a named calorie-tracking app from full install data.
- No randomised study was found that manipulates first-week logging frequency (for example by assigning a logging target) and measures later retention.
- The specific "Lose It! logging-consistency" study the brief had in mind was not pinned down; the Farage and JMIR 2018 details above are snippet-level and should be checked before quoting.
- I could not open the 2026 JMIR mHealth scoping review; its figures need verifying.

## 3. What companies say predicts long-term retention in the first days

### Takeaway
Company figures cluster around the same idea: users who reach a small early threshold (a 7-day streak, four logged days in week one, a reminder set, a first social interaction) retain several times better. Almost all of these are correlations among self-selected users; only Duolingo publishes A/B results, and its measured causal lifts are small single-digit percentages, not multiples.

### Cited Findings

Duolingo (the best-documented case; A/B tested unless stated):

- Learners who reach a 7-day streak are 2.4 times more likely to use Duolingo the next day than learners without a streak. Correlation. Published 19 November 2020. [opened] — [Duolingo blog, "Improving the streak"](https://blog.duolingo.com/improving-the-streak/)
- Separating the streak from the daily XP goal, so one lesson extends the streak (A/B test): day-14 retention +3.3% relative; daily active learners +1% relative; share of daily learners on a streak +10.5% relative within 20 days, and +19% for new learners. Trade-off: fewer learners hit their daily goal. A year later, just over half of daily learners had a streak of 7 days or more, up from about a third (before and after, not tested); the change raised learners on a 7-plus-day streak by "over 40%". [opened] — [Duolingo blog, "Improving the streak"](https://blog.duolingo.com/improving-the-streak/)
- Almost 40% of learners active two days running without a streak had chosen the highest ("intense") daily goal, the observation that prompted the change: an ambitious self-set goal was stopping people from getting a streak. [opened] — [Duolingo blog](https://blog.duolingo.com/improving-the-streak/)
- New streak-extension and milestone animations: +1.7% in the likelihood that a brand-new learner was still using Duolingo 7 days later. Allowing two Streak Freezes to be equipped: +0.38% relative daily active learners. Over 6 million people were on a streak of 7 days or more (January 2022). Learners who reach a 7-day streak are 3.6 times more likely to complete their course (correlation). Published 31 January 2022; the test method is not stated for each figure. [opened] — [Duolingo blog, "The habit-building research behind your Duolingo streak"](https://blog.duolingo.com/how-duolingo-streak-builds-habit/)
- Jorge Mazal (former CPO): the team modelled retention and found that current user retention rate (CURR: active today and at least once in the prior 6 days) had about 5 times the effect on daily active users of the next-best lever. Over four years CURR rose 21%, equal to cutting daily churn of the best users by over 40%, and daily active users grew 4.5 times. Leaderboards raised overall learning time 17% and tripled the number of highly engaged learners (an hour a day, 5 days a week), with significant gains in day-1 and day-7 retention. The share of daily users with a 7-plus-day streak "increased almost 3 times to more than half". Users who reached a 10-day streak dropped off substantially less, which Mazal himself flags as possible selection bias. A streak-saver notification was the "first big win"; no figure given. A copied "moves counter" game mechanic had a neutral result. Push volume was deliberately capped; changes needed strong justification. Referral programme: +3% new users. [opened] — [Lenny's Newsletter, "How Duolingo reignited user growth"](https://www.lennysnewsletter.com/p/how-duolingo-reignited-user-growth)
- Gina Gotthilf (former VP growth), First Round Review: moving sign-up later ("soft wall", letting people try a lesson first) increased daily active users by about 20%, with later refinements adding 8.2%. Weekend Amulet (streak protection over a weekend): +2.1% day-7 and +4% day-14 retention. In-app coach: +7.2% day-14 retention. Badges v1: +2.4% daily active users. Personalised reminder copy: +5% daily active users. Reminder timing was set at 23.5 hours after the last session. [opened] — [First Round Review](https://review.firstround.com/the-tenets-of-a-b-testing-from-duolingos-master-growth-hacker/)
- Jackson Shuttleworth (streaks lead), Lenny's podcast: over 600 streak experiments; "retention is most fragile in the first seven days"; changing a button from "continue" to "commit to my goal" "significantly increased retention" (no figure on the page). [opened, summary page only] — [Lenny's Newsletter, "Behind the product: Duolingo streaks"](https://www.lennysnewsletter.com/p/behind-the-product-duolingo-streaks)
- Streak wager (bet in-app currency on keeping a streak 7 more days): secondary teardowns report +14% retention, placed at day 7 in the source I found, though the figure is widely repeated as day 14. Not in any Duolingo primary post retrieved; treat as unverified. The same teardowns claim 32 million daily users with a 7-plus-day streak and that Streak Freeze cut churn 21% among at-risk users; neither is confirmed by Duolingo. [snippet] — [Yu-kai Chou teardown](https://yukaichou.com/gamification-study/master-the-art-of-streak-design-for-short-term-engagement-and-long-term-success/); [Deconstructor of Fun teardown](https://duolingo.deconstructoroffun.com/mechanics/streaks)
- A third-party roundup says Duolingo reports over 5 million users with a streak of a year or more. Not found in a shareholder letter. [snippet] — [SQ Magazine](https://sqmagazine.co.uk/duolingo-statistics.md)

MyFitnessPal (company claims, correlational):

- Users who logged food on at least four days in their first week were "seven times more likely to make measurable progress" towards their weight-loss goal. Internal data described as preliminary; no sample size or baseline published; the article notes independent data scientists' objection that early loggers may simply be more motivated. Reported 30 December 2024. Also: nearly 50% of subscribers who used the app daily reached a 5% weight-loss goal within 16 weeks (premium 49.3%, non-premium 46.9%). [opened] — [Athletech News](https://athletechnews.com/?p=118545)
- Friends: users who shared their food diary with friends lost twice as much weight (2014 press); users with friends on the app lose about twice as much; users with 10 or more friends lose four times as much, and those with at least 10 friends active in their first 30 days lost an average of 22.75 lb. Company analyses, described inconsistently across outlets, no methods published. [snippet] — [Lifehacker Australia 2014](https://www.lifehacker.com.au/2014/05/you-may-have-greater-success-losing-weight-with-a-friend/); [Active.com](https://www.active.com/fitness/articles/5-habits-that-lead-to-weight-loss); [MyFitnessPal blog](https://blog.myfitnesspal.com/have-you-joined-a-fitness-tribe-yet-heres-why-you-should/)
- Streaks: MyFitnessPal has made logging streaks more visible and later let users fix missed days to keep a streak alive; no retention figure published. [snippet] — [BriefGlance](https://briefglance.com/articles/myfitnesspals-first-four-bets-on-data-to-build-lasting-health-habits)

Calm (vendor case study):

- A daily reminder setting was buried in Settings and used by under 1% of users; those users retained almost 3 times better. Calm then prompted new users to set a reminder after their first session: 40% opted in and showed the same roughly 3 times lift, and the prompt was rolled out. Amplitude staff say the lift persisted 12 or more weeks out. No sample size or significance published; the source is Amplitude's marketing. [snippet] — [Amplitude case study](https://www.amplitude.com/case-studies/calm); [Amplitude blog](https://amplitude.com/blog/calm-increased-retention-amplitude)

Strava and others:

- Strava's own analysis, reported second-hand: users who gave kudos, rode with others or joined clubs in their first weeks were more than twice as likely to be active six months later. Primary report not found. Correlation. [snippet] — [Bicycling](https://www.bicycling.co.za/training/social-secret-maintaining-fitness-habit/?amp=1)
- Peloton's CEO: churn is 60% lower for subscriptions where members use two or more disciplines a month versus one. Company claim, correlation. [snippet] — [PYMNTS](https://www.pymnts.com/earnings/2025/peloton-continues-comeback-with-strong-subscription-metrics/)

### Inferences
- The gap between the correlational multiples (2.4 times, 3 times, 7 times) and Duolingo's tested lifts (+0.38% to +4% per change, +14% at most and unverified) is the size of the selection effect. A realistic expectation for any single first-week device is a low single-digit percentage improvement in day-7 or day-14 retention; the multiples describe who self-selects, not what the feature does.
- The Calm result is commonly cited as causal, but the published comparison is still between people who chose to set a reminder and people who declined, inside the prompted group. What can be said is that prompting raised reminder adoption from under 1% to 40% and that Calm judged the overall effect worth shipping.
- Duolingo's most transferable finding is structural: lowering the bar for "today counts" (one lesson, not a full XP goal) raised retention, and ambitious self-chosen goals were suppressing streaks. For a food diary the analogue is that one logged item, not a complete day, should keep the day alive.
- Duolingo's second transferable finding is delayed sign-up (+20% daily active users). Ember requires sign-in before use by the owner's decision, so this is a known cost to weigh, not a recommendation to reverse it.

### Gaps
- No reliable published figures were found for Fitbit (the often-quoted "friends take more steps" claim), Apple Fitness ring closing, or any Strava primary source; I searched for Strava and Peloton, not specifically for Fitbit and Apple, so those remain unverified rather than absent.
- No Duolingo figures were found for the home-screen widget or for what share of users return after losing a streak.
- No published Noom or Lose It! company figure on first-days predictors was retrieved.
- MyFitnessPal's own blog post ("MyFitnessPal works if you use it") returned 403 and was not read.

## 4. Evidence on specific early-experience devices: goals, reminder times, push opt-in, checklists, gamification, social features, streaks

### Takeaway
The randomised evidence supports modest, real effects for goal setting, gamification and social comparison while they are running, and shows those effects shrink once the mechanic is removed. For streaks, experiments show that a visibly broken streak reduces later engagement and that a way to repair or protect the streak removes most of that harm. Evidence for push opt-in, reminder-time prompts and first-session checklists is vendor or company material only.

### Cited Findings

Goal setting:

- **Epton, Currie and Armitage 2017, J Consult Clin Psychol, meta-analysis:** 141 papers, 384 effect sizes, N = 16,523. Goal setting had a unique positive effect on behaviour; it worked best when the goal was difficult, set publicly, or a group goal. A pooled d = 0.34 (CI 0.28 to 0.41) is attributed to this paper by a secondary index whose citation was mismatched; check the full text before quoting the number. [snippet] — [Epton et al. 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29189034/)
- **Duolingo, company A/B and observation:** asking users to commit ("commit to my goal" button) "significantly increased retention" (no figure); but almost 40% of two-day users with no streak had picked the hardest daily goal, and decoupling the streak from that goal raised day-14 retention 3.3%. [opened] — [Lenny's Newsletter](https://www.lennysnewsletter.com/p/behind-the-product-duolingo-streaks); [Duolingo blog](https://blog.duolingo.com/improving-the-streak/)
- **MyFitnessPal weight-goal data:** logging more calories per day in the first 7 days after setting a goal went with a lower chance of meeting it. Observational. [snippet] — [PMC7197296](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7197296)

Reminder time and push notifications:

- **Calm:** prompting for a reminder time after the first session took adoption from under 1% to 40%; reminder-setters retained about 3 times better. Vendor case study, see section 3. [snippet] — [Amplitude](https://www.amplitude.com/case-studies/calm)
- **Duolingo:** reminders sent 23.5 hours after the last session (that is, at the time the person used the app yesterday); personalised copy +5% daily active users; push volume capped by policy. Company A/B. [opened] — [First Round Review](https://review.firstround.com/the-tenets-of-a-b-testing-from-duolingos-master-growth-hacker/); [Lenny's Newsletter](https://www.lennysnewsletter.com/p/how-duolingo-reignited-user-growth)
- **Airship opt-in rates:** push opt-in 64.0% on Android and 49.4% on iOS (period not confirmed); Airship's opt-in benchmark is a monthly rate averaged over 12 months. Vendor benchmark. [snippet] — [Airship](https://www.airship.com/blog/a-marketers-guide-to-push-notification-benchmarks/)
- **Urban Airship, users who first opened apps in September 2016:** users who received any push in their first 90 days had retention nearly 3 times higher (190%) than the average of opted-out and zero-send users; users who opted in but were sent nothing retained worse than opted-out users on both platforms. Observational, from a push vendor, old. [snippet; the exact Airship page was not opened] — [Airship](https://www.airship.com/blog/push-notification-strategy-customer-retention/)
- **Reasons for abandonment include "annoying notifications"** (Kidman et al. 2024), and a 2026 interview study (26 mHealth users managing diet and exercise relapse) found reminders and feedback trigger guilt and shame when goals are missed, which can lead to disengagement. [opened / snippet] — [Kidman et al. 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11694054/); [JMIR mHealth 2026;e94792](https://mhealth.jmir.org/2026/1/e94792/PDF)

Gamification and early rewards:

- **Mazeas et al. 2022, JMIR, meta-analysis of RCTs:** 16 studies, 2,407 participants. Gamified interventions increased physical activity, Hedges g = 0.42 (95% CI 0.14 to 0.69). At follow-up averaging 14 weeks after the intervention ended the effect was g = 0.15 (0.07 to 0.23). Against inactive controls g = 0.58; against active non-gamified controls g = 0.23 (0.05 to 0.41). [snippet] — [Mazeas et al. 2022](https://www.jmir.org/2022/1/e26779)
- **Johnson et al. 2016, Internet Interventions, systematic review:** 19 papers (the PMC highlights say 21): 59% reported positive effects, 41% mixed, mostly moderate or lower quality evidence; clearer for behaviour than for cognition; few studies compared a gamified version against the same thing without gamification. [snippet] — [Johnson et al. 2016](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6096297)
- **STEP UP, Patel et al. 2019, JAMA Internal Medicine, RCT:** 602 adults with elevated BMI from 40 US states; 24-week intervention, 12-week follow-up; points and levels for step goals, in support, collaboration or competition arms versus control. Extra daily steps versus control during the intervention: competition +920 (95% CI 513 to 1,328), support +689, collaboration +637. After the game was removed activity fell in all arms and only the competition arm stayed significantly above control. [snippet] — [Patel et al. 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6735420)
- **Duolingo:** badges +2.4% daily active users; milestone animations +1.7% day-7 retention for new learners; leaderboards +17% learning time. Company A/B. [opened] — [First Round Review](https://review.firstround.com/the-tenets-of-a-b-testing-from-duolingos-master-growth-hacker/); [Duolingo blog](https://blog.duolingo.com/how-duolingo-streak-builds-habit/); [Lenny's Newsletter](https://www.lennysnewsletter.com/p/how-duolingo-reignited-user-growth)

Social accountability, groups and leaderboards:

- **Althoff, Jindal and Leskovec 2017 (WSDM), Azumio Argus activity tracker:** 791 million actions from about 6 million users over 5 years. Joining the in-app social network and adding connections was associated with +30% in-app activity, +17% retention and +7% physical activity (about 400 steps a day; the authors' slides say about 350). The effect diminished over about 20 weeks and with each further connection, and was larger for friends than followers. Quasi-experimental (natural experiment, difference-in-differences, matching), not randomised. [snippet] — [Althoff et al. 2017](https://arxiv.org/abs/1612.03053v1)
- **STEP UP:** competition (a small-group leaderboard) outperformed support and collaboration and was the only arm whose effect survived follow-up. RCT. [snippet] — [Patel et al. 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6735420)
- **Epton et al. 2017:** public and group goals strengthen the goal-setting effect. Meta-analysis. [snippet] — [Epton et al. 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29189034/)
- **Company correlations:** MyFitnessPal friends (2 to 4 times the weight loss), Strava early kudos or clubs (twice as likely active at six months). See section 3.

Streaks, broken streaks and forgiving streaks:

- **Silverman and Barasch 2023, Journal of Consumer Research, seven studies (experiments):** showing an intact streak in a behaviour log increases later engagement compared with showing a broken streak, independent of what the person actually did; the effect depends only on how the log represents it. The harm of a broken streak is larger when people blame themselves and is attenuated when they can "repair" the streak. The authors suggest apps should not draw attention to broken streaks and should offer other ways to keep a streak going. [snippet] — [University of Colorado summary](https://www.colorado.edu/business/faculty-research/2023/04/19/or-track-how-broken-streaks-affect-consumer-decisions); [paper record](https://udspace.udel.edu/items/42ce576b-8e1f-429a-8541-e29e48dbcfbb); [UDaily](https://udel.edu/udaily/2024/march/power-of-streaks-motivation-jackie-silverman)
- **Sharif and Shu 2019, Organizational Behavior and Human Decision Processes:** "Nudging persistence after failure through emergency reserves": building explicit slack into a goal (a reserve that can be used in an emergency) is proposed as a way to keep people persisting after a slip. Duolingo cites this line of UPenn and UCLA work as the rationale for Streak Freeze. Findings not read in full. [snippet] — [Sharif and Shu 2019](https://anderson-review.ucla.edu/wp-content/uploads/2021/03/Sharif-Shu_EmergencyReserveFailure_OBHDP2019.pdf); [Duolingo blog](https://blog.duolingo.com/how-duolingo-streak-builds-habit/)
- **Duolingo forgiving-streak tests:** two Streak Freezes +0.38% daily active learners; Weekend Amulet +2.1% day-7 and +4% day-14 retention; one-lesson streak +3.3% day-14 retention. Company A/B. [opened] — sources as in section 3.
- **UCL and Loughborough study, October 2025, of social media posts about five fitness and calorie apps:** losing a streak or missing a daily goal contributed to avoidant behaviour for some users; users became preoccupied with targets and streaks and some appeared to give up when they could not hit them. Based on posts, so it cannot say how common this is. [snippet; UCL page returned 403] — [UCL News](https://www.ucl.ac.uk/news/2025/oct/emotional-strain-fitness-and-calorie-counting-apps-revealed); [Athletech News](https://athletechnews.com/popular-fitness-apps-may-reduce-motivation-study/)
- **Qualitative study of 17 recreational runners who ended long streaks (2026):** sadness, anger, disappointment and relief afterwards; some had run injured to protect the streak; most later started a new streak. [snippet] — [University of Stirling](https://www.stir.ac.uk/research/hub/file/2261489)
- **Lally et al. 2010:** missing a single day did not significantly affect habit formation; repeated misses did. See section 5. [snippet] — [UCL](https://www.ucl.ac.uk/news/2009/aug/how-long-does-it-take-form-habit)

### Inferences
- The streak evidence lines up from three directions (consumer experiments, habit research, Duolingo's tests): a streak helps while intact, a visible break is the dangerous moment, and forgiveness (a freeze, a repair, a held streak) recovers engagement without evidence of harm. A streak that can be held through a break, which Ember already has, is the evidence-backed form.
- Gamification effects are real but roughly two thirds of the effect disappears within about 14 weeks of removing the mechanic (g 0.42 to 0.15), and the effect against a non-gamified active control is about half the headline (g 0.23). XP and quests should be expected to support the first weeks, not to carry month three on their own.
- Competition in small groups is the one social mechanic with randomised evidence of an effect that outlasts the intervention; open leaderboards among strangers are not what was tested.
- The self-set goal is double-edged: goal setting helps (meta-analysis), harder goals help more in trials, yet in product data ambitious goals blocked streaks and early high calorie logs went with missed goals. The reconciliation is to let the outcome goal be ambitious while keeping the daily "counts as done" bar very low.
- Push evidence is circular: vendors report that people who receive pushes retain better, but people who opt in are already more committed. The only defensible claims are that opt-in is roughly half of iOS users, that asking at a moment of demonstrated value raises opt-in (Calm: 40% after the first session), and that badly judged notifications are a named reason for quitting.

### Gaps
- No controlled evidence was found for first-session checklists or progress bars in health apps. Nothing on the endowed-progress effect was retrieved; I did not search for it specifically.
- No independent (non-vendor) randomised evidence was found linking push opt-in or reminder-time selection to day-7 or day-30 retention.
- No study was found that measures what proportion of app users quit after losing a streak, or that compares a forgiving streak with a strict one in a health app.
- The Epton pooled effect size and the full Sharif and Shu results need checking against the papers.
- Evidence on leaderboards harming lower-ranked users was not searched in depth.

## 5. Habit formation, fresh starts and when to prompt

### Takeaway
Habits take months, not a week: the best estimates are a median of about two months with a range from days to most of a year, and gym-going specifically takes months. A first week therefore cannot form the habit; its job is to fix a stable cue and get enough repetitions for the behaviour to start feeling easier, with tolerance for a missed day.

### Cited Findings
- **Lally et al. 2010, European Journal of Social Psychology:** 96 volunteers chose a new daily health behaviour tied to a daily cue and reported for 84 days; 82 finished. Median time to reach a plateau of automaticity was 66 days, range 18 to a forecast 254 days; about half had not reached habit strength by day 84. Missing one opportunity did not significantly affect the process; people who were very inconsistent did not form habits. Automaticity was self-reported. Figures from UCL's and the BPS's summaries, not the paper. [snippet] — [UCL News](https://www.ucl.ac.uk/news/2009/aug/how-long-does-it-take-form-habit); [BPS Research Digest](https://bps.org.uk/research-digest/how-form-habit)
- **Singh et al. 2024, Healthcare, systematic review and meta-analysis:** 20 studies, 2,601 participants. Median time to habit formation 59 to 66 days; means 106 to 154 days; individual range 4 to 335 days. Frequency, timing, type of habit, personal choice and enjoyment were associated with habit strength; morning practices and self-selected habits tended to be stronger. Small evidence base, mostly flossing and diet. [snippet] — [Singh et al. 2024](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39685110/); [full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11641623/)
- **Buyalskaya et al. 2023, PNAS:** machine learning on more than 30,000 gym members of 24 Hour Fitness over four years and more than 3,000 hospital workers' handwashing over nearly 100 shifts. Gym habits took months to form (Caltech's summary: about six months on average); handwashing took weeks. There is "no magic number". Time of day did not affect gym habit formation; past behaviour did. Gym-goers whose behaviour was more predictable responded less to an intervention meant to raise attendance. Observational. [snippet] — [Buyalskaya et al. 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10151500); [Caltech](https://neuroscience.caltech.edu/news/no-magic-number-for-time-it-takes-to-form-habits); [ScienceDaily](https://sciencedaily.com/releases/2023/04/230417155750.htm)
- **Dai, Milkman and Riis 2014, Management Science, "The Fresh Start Effect":** three archival field studies. Gym visits by University of Pennsylvania undergraduates, diet-related searches and commitment contracts all rise after temporal landmarks: the start of a week, month, year or term, a birthday, a holiday. One effect size given in coverage: the rise in gym-going after a birthday equals the effect of keeping the gym open two extra hours; 21st birthdays were an exception. Observational. [snippet] — [paper PDF](https://faculty.wharton.upenn.edu/wp-content/uploads/2014/06/Dai_Fresh_Start_2014_Mgmt_Sci.pdf); [Scientific American](https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/is-it-the-right-time-for-a-fresh-start/)
- **Duolingo practice consistent with cue stability:** reminders are sent 23.5 hours after the previous session, that is, at the time of day the person last chose to use the app. Company practice. [opened] — [First Round Review](https://review.firstround.com/the-tenets-of-a-b-testing-from-duolingos-master-growth-hacker/)
- **Seasonality:** fitness apps show a January spike in installs and the largest seasonal swing of any category. [snippet] — [UXCam](https://uxcam.com/blog/mobile-app-retention-benchmarks/)

### Inferences
- Simple, frequent behaviours tied to an existing cue (handwashing: weeks) automate far faster than effortful ones (gym: months). Logging a meal sits nearer the simple end if it is quick and attached to eating; a training plan sits at the slow end. The first week should concentrate on the fast-forming behaviour.
- "21 days" has no support. Any copy that promises a habit in a week or three weeks would be an unsupported claim; "about two months for most people, and it varies a lot" is what the evidence supports.
- A single missed day does not damage habit formation (Lally), while a visibly broken streak damages motivation (Silverman and Barasch). The psychological cost of a miss is therefore created by how the app displays it, which argues for forgiving display.
- Fresh-start evidence supports timing prompts and restarts to Mondays, the first of the month, the new year and birthdays, and offering a clean restart after a lapse framed as a new beginning. It is observational, so the size of any effect in an app is unknown.
- Self-chosen habits and self-chosen times were stronger in the review, which supports asking the person when they want to be reminded rather than assigning a time; Buyalskaya found time of day itself did not matter, so consistency of the cue matters more than which hour.

### Gaps
- No figures were retrieved for implementation intentions (if-then planning; the usual citation is Gollwitzer and Sheeran 2006) and I did not search for them; the claim that if-then plans improve follow-through is therefore unsourced here.
- Exact percentage lifts for start-of-week, month and year in Dai et al. are in the paper's tables and were not retrieved.
- The Lally figures are from institutional summaries, not the paper.
- No study was found that tests fresh-start timing of prompts inside a diet or fitness app.

## 6. The cost of friction in calorie tracking: does faster logging improve adherence, and why people quit

### Takeaway
Effort is the most consistently reported reason people stop food logging, alongside inaccurate crowd-sourced databases, fading motivation and negative emotion. But there is no good trial evidence that photo, barcode or voice logging improves adherence; the one controlled comparison found photo logging was harder and less sustained than text, contrary to what participants expected.

### Cited Findings

Why people quit:

- **Kidman et al. 2024 scoping review:** 22 distinct reasons for abandoning lifestyle and mental health apps in 6 groups: technical problems (app stops working, data loss, mandatory account creation); privacy; poor user experience (not effective, not engaging, confusing, annoying notifications); missing content or features (personalisation, accountability, variety, professional advice); time and money (time to enter data, hidden costs); and changing needs (goal achieved, fading motivation, boredom, switched app). Most often cited across studies: "confusing or difficult" (4 studies), "I achieved my fitness goal" (3), "fading motivation" (3). These are counts of studies, not shares of users. Diet apps had 86% abandonment. [opened] — [Kidman et al. 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11694054/)
- **Cordeiro et al. (University of Washington; survey of 141 current and lapsed food journalers plus 5,526 forum posts from three food-journal communities):** the effort of journaling is a major barrier; user-built food databases are unreliable ("not all entries can be considered accurate"); journals can create feelings of judgment and nudge people towards easy-to-log packaged food. [snippet; year given inconsistently as 2015 or 2016 and the paper was not opened] — [PMC record](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4755274); [Big Think coverage](https://bigthink.com/surprising-science/why-so-many-people-quit-food-logging-apps-in-the-first-week/)
- **Helander et al. 2014:** 2.58% of 189,770 users of a photo food journal became active users. [snippet] — [Helander et al. 2014](https://www.jmir.org/2014/4/e109/)
- **UCL and Loughborough, October 2025:** posts about five fitness and calorie apps describe shame, discouragement and giving up when calorie targets or streaks were missed. Cannot establish prevalence. [snippet] — [UCL News](https://www.ucl.ac.uk/news/2025/oct/emotional-strain-fitness-and-calorie-counting-apps-revealed)
- **JMIR mHealth 2026 interview study (26 users):** relapse is experienced as guilt, shame and loss of meaning; reminders and feedback after missed goals can deepen disengagement. [snippet] — [JMIR mHealth 2026;e94792](https://mhealth.jmir.org/2026/1/e94792/PDF)
- **JMIR 2025 model-development study:** describes adherence to dietary self-monitoring as waning because it is labour-intensive and has no efficient passive recording method. Authors' framing, not a measured result. [snippet] — [JMIR 2025;e65431](https://jmir.org/2025/1/e65431/PDF)
- **A 2022 ECIS study of weight-loss app reviews** identified nine reasons users stop. Details not retrieved. [snippet] — [ECIS paper](https://core.ac.uk/download/511301835.pdf)

Does faster logging help?

- **Silverman, Barasch, Diehl and Zauberman 2022, Journal of the Association for Consumer Research, "Harder Than You Think":** in a week-long field study (425 participants who wanted to monitor or change their eating), people assigned to photo logging found it harder and less enjoyable than text logging, logged less of what they ate and were less willing to continue, although people predicted in advance that photos would be easier. Authors suggest offering both in one app. Randomised field experiment; exact drop-off percentages are behind a paywall. [snippet] — [paper](https://www.journals.uchicago.edu/doi/10.1086/720444); [Yale News](https://news.yale.edu/2022/09/15/when-counting-calories-words-are-more-valuable-pictures)
- **PIQNIQ RCT 2021 (n = 132):** compared simultaneous entry, photo-assisted recall and 24-hour recall for accuracy, not adherence; added sugars were overestimated by more than 30% in both app groups. [snippet] — [PubMed 33693732](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33693732/)
- **Digital versus paper:** a systematic review reported that adherence to self-monitoring with digital tools was better than with paper. [snippet, review not identified precisely] — [Raber et al. 2021](https://dx.doi.org/10.1017/S136898002100358X)
- **Low bar is enough:** in Payne et al. completeness of logs did not predict weight loss while frequency and consistency did; in Turner-McGrievy et al. days with two or more eating occasions logged was the best marker. Observational. [opened / snippet] — [Payne et al. 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9159560); [Turner-McGrievy et al. 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31155473/)
- **Vendor claim to discount:** a photo-logging app's blog says AI photo users lose 38% more weight at 12 weeks than manual trackers, with no trial cited. Marketing. [snippet] — [Nutrola blog](https://nutrola.app/en/blog/average-weight-loss-by-calorie-tracking-method-photo-vs-manual-vs-barcode)

### Inferences
- The claim "faster logging improves adherence" is plausible and matches every qualitative account of why people stop, but it is not demonstrated. The one experiment contradicts the intuition for photos (2022, before current AI recognition, where the photo was the record and not an input to automatic entry). Any statement in the report should say that the effect of barcode, AI photo and voice entry on retention is untested in published trials.
- People mispredict which method they will stick with, so asking in onboarding which logging method someone prefers is weak evidence of what will work for them; offering several methods and watching which one each person actually uses is better supported.
- Three quit reasons are directly addressable in a first week: the time cost of entry, distrust of numbers (database accuracy, which supports clear Estimate, Recalled and Label marking), and guilt after a miss.
- "Mandatory account creation" appears as a named abandonment reason in the review. This is relevant to the sign-in-first decision and is worth stating plainly alongside Duolingo's delayed-sign-up result.
- "I achieved my goal" is a legitimate reason for leaving and inflates churn figures; not all attrition is failure.

### Gaps
- No randomised or even observational study was found comparing barcode scanning, AI photo recognition or voice entry with manual search for adherence or retention. Peer-reviewed evidence on AI-era photo logging (2023 onwards) and adherence was not found; one 2025 arXiv evaluation (SnappyMeal) surfaced in search but was not read.
- No survey giving percentages of users who quit calorie apps for each reason was found; the available evidence is qualitative or counts of studies.
- The Cordeiro paper and the ECIS review study were not opened; a validation study reporting that 74.8% (101 of 135) of participants preferred an image-based tool (Keenoa) appeared in search results but I could not confirm its link, so it is not cited above.

## 7. Does onboarding length or personalisation itself affect day-7 or day-30 retention, separately from conversion?

### Takeaway
No rigorous published evidence isolates the effect of onboarding length or personalisation on day-7 or day-30 retention. The one decent experiment found a shorter flow raised completion but lowered trial starts, with no retention measured; everything else is an uncontrolled case study or a vendor opinion.

### Cited Findings
- **Everlove (couples app, 150,000-plus users), RevenueCat blog, test started 14 June 2026, 24,000 users, randomised:** 28-step versus 19-step onboarding. Completion 78.10% versus 86.70% (+8.6 points, strong significance); paywall reach 74.26% versus 83.92%; trial start among paywall viewers 3.83% versus 3.13% (18% relative fall, strong significance); initial conversion of registered users 3.35% versus 3.11% (not significant); average lifetime value per registered user $0.1163 versus $0.1592 (+36.9%, weak, p = 0.093). Day-1, day-7 and day-30 retention were not reported. The author's conclusion: "onboarding length isn't the first thing to decide"; find where the funnel leaks first. Not a health app. [opened] — [RevenueCat blog](https://www.revenuecat.com/blog/growth/everlove-onboarding-experiment)
- **Duolingo, company A/B:** letting people try the product before sign-up increased daily active users about 20%, later refinements a further 8.2%. This is about where sign-up sits, not length. [opened] — [First Round Review](https://review.firstround.com/the-tenets-of-a-b-testing-from-duolingos-master-growth-hacker/)
- **Sunnyside (alcohol moderation app), Braze case study, 2020:** a sign-up diagnostic of more than 30 screens is credited with 85%-plus first-week retention. Uncontrolled vendor case study; the retention definition is not comparable with install-based benchmarks. [snippet] — [Braze](https://www.braze.com/resources/articles/how-cutback-coach-achieved-85-first-week-retention-through-personalized-onboarding)
- **Murror, founder's post:** one personalisation prompt on day 1 reportedly lifted day-7 retention from 23% to 41%. Self-reported, no control group. [snippet] — [Product Hunt](https://www.producthunt.com/p/murror/the-onboarding-trick-that-doubled-our-day-7-retention)
- **Master's thesis (DiVA):** onboarding improvements raised activation, but 7-day retention moved only from 17% to 18%. [snippet] — [DiVA](https://www.diva-portal.org/smash/get/diva2:1985428/FULLTEXT01.pdf)
- **Adapty (paywall vendor) opinion:** long flows of 25 to 30 screens hold up in health and fitness when each screen ties to a real user problem; utility apps need 3 to 5. No data shown. [snippet] — [Adapty](https://adapty.io/blog/how-to-personalize-onboarding-and-paywalls-in-your-mobile-app/)
- **Adjacent peer-reviewed signals:** lack of personalisation and mandatory account creation are both named reasons for abandonment (Kidman et al. 2024); self-selected habits are stronger than assigned ones (Singh et al. 2024). [opened / snippet] — [Kidman et al. 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11694054/); [Singh et al. 2024](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39685110/)

### Inferences
- Long quiz-style onboarding in health and fitness is a monetisation pattern: its documented effect is on trial starts at a paywall, and the Everlove data show it costs completion. For a free product with no paywall at the end of onboarding, the measured benefit of length disappears and only the completion cost remains, unless the answers are used to make the first session better.
- The defensible position is that onboarding questions earn their place only where the answer changes what the person sees in the first session (targets, reminder time, logging method), since personalisation, not length, is what the abandonment literature points to.
- Since no one has published retention results by onboarding length, this is a question an app has to answer with its own cohorts: completion rate, first log within the first session, and day-7 return are the three numbers to compare.

### Gaps
- No experiment was found, in any app category, that randomises onboarding length or personalisation and reports day-7 or day-30 retention.
- No peer-reviewed study of onboarding design in diet or fitness apps was found.
- Published accounts from Noom, MyFitnessPal, Lose It! or other calorie apps of their own onboarding experiments were not found.
