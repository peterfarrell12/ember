# Rules and risks for onboarding a calorie and fitness app in Ireland and the UK (position at 26 November 2026)

Fact file, not legal advice. Quotes are short and exact where marked with quotation marks; some primary pages could only be read in fragments (noted). "Unverified" means I could not confirm the point against a primary source in this session. Where the law is unsettled or a specialist should be asked, the Gaps sub-section says so.

## 1. Apple App Store: Review Guidelines, Human Interface Guidelines and recent enforcement

### Takeaway
Apple's current text allows a sign-in wall only where the app has "significant account-based features", requires in-app account deletion, requires explicit permission before personal data goes to "third-party AI", and bars health data from advertising use. The April 2026 Cal AI removal was about paywall and billing design (in-app purchase bypass, weekly-price prominence, a trial toggle hiding renewal, a second offer after decline), not about calorie advice as such.

### Cited Findings
Sign-in, accounts, data minimisation
- 5.1.1(v) Account Sign-In: "If your app doesn't include significant account-based features, let people use it without a login. If your app supports account creation, you must also offer account deletion within the app. Apps may not require users to enter personal information to function, except when directly relevant to the core functionality of the app or required by law." — [Apple App Review Guidelines](https://developer.apple.com/app-store/review/guidelines/)
- 4.8 Login Services applies only to apps that use "a third-party or social login service (such as Facebook Login, Google Sign-In ...)"; an equivalent privacy-preserving login is not required if "Your app exclusively uses your company's own account setup and sign-in systems." — [Apple App Review Guidelines](https://developer.apple.com/app-store/review/guidelines/)
- 5.1.1(iii) Data Minimization: "Apps should only request access to data relevant to the core functionality of the app and should only collect and use data that is required to accomplish the relevant task." — [Apple App Review Guidelines](https://developer.apple.com/app-store/review/guidelines/)
- 5.1.1(x): apps may request basic contact information "so long as the request is optional for the user, features and services are not conditional on providing the information". — [Apple App Review Guidelines](https://developer.apple.com/app-store/review/guidelines/)
- 5.1.1(ix): apps "that require sensitive user information should be submitted by a legal entity that provides the services, and not by an individual developer." — [Apple App Review Guidelines](https://developer.apple.com/app-store/review/guidelines/)
- 5.1.1(i): the privacy policy must be linked in App Store Connect and "within the app in an easily accessible manner", and must "Explain its data retention/deletion policies and describe how a user can revoke consent and/or request deletion of the user's data." — [Apple App Review Guidelines](https://developer.apple.com/app-store/review/guidelines/)

Permissions and consent
- 5.1.1(ii) Permission: "Apps that collect user or usage data must secure user consent for the collection ... Apps must also provide the customer with an easily accessible and understandable way to withdraw consent. Ensure your purpose strings clearly and completely describe your use of the data." — [Apple App Review Guidelines](https://developer.apple.com/app-store/review/guidelines/)
- 5.1.1(iv) Access: apps must "not attempt to manipulate, trick, or force people to consent to unnecessary data access ... Where possible, provide alternative solutions for users who don't grant consent." — [Apple App Review Guidelines](https://developer.apple.com/app-store/review/guidelines/)
- 5.1.2(i): "You must clearly disclose where personal data will be shared with third parties, including with third-party AI, and obtain explicit permission before doing so." The same rule says "Your app may not require users to enable system functionalities (such as push notifications, location services, tracking) in order to access functionality". — [Apple App Review Guidelines](https://developer.apple.com/app-store/review/guidelines/)
- The "third-party AI" wording was added in the guideline update that Apple announced in November 2025 (reported live 13 November 2025). Apple does not define "third-party AI" and commentators disagree on scope. — [Apple developer news](https://developer.apple.com/news/?id=ey6d8onl); [TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2025/11/13/apples-new-app-review-guidelines-clamp-down-on-apps-sharing-personal-data-with-third-party-ai)
- HIG, Privacy: "Request permission only when your app clearly needs access to the data or resource." "Avoid requesting permission at launch unless the data or resource is required for your app to function." Purpose strings: "Aim for a brief, complete sentence that's straightforward, specific, and easy to understand." — [Apple HIG: Privacy](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/privacy)
- HIG, pre-alert screens: "Include only one button and make it clear that it opens the system alert." "Use a term like "Continue" or "Next" to title the single button". "don't create a button title that uses "Allow" or similar terms, because people don't allow anything in a pre-alert screen." "Don't include additional actions in your custom screen or window, unless needed to obtain a legal consent." — [Apple HIG: Privacy](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/privacy)
- HIG, Onboarding: "if onboarding is necessary, design a flow that's fast, fun, and optional." "Postpone nonessential setup flows or customization steps." "Provide reasonable default settings so most people can immediately start interacting with your app". "Avoid displaying licensing details within your onboarding flow." Permission requests belong in onboarding only "If your app or game needs access to private data or resources before it can function"; "Otherwise, present a permission request when people first access the specific function". — [Apple HIG: Onboarding](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/onboarding)

Health data
- 5.1.3(i): apps "may not use or disclose to third parties data gathered in the health, fitness, and medical research context—including from the ... HealthKit API, Motion and Fitness ... for advertising, marketing, or other use-based data mining purposes other than improving health management, or for the purpose of health research, and then only with permission." "You must disclose the specific health data that you are collecting from the device." — [Apple App Review Guidelines](https://developer.apple.com/app-store/review/guidelines/)
- 5.1.3(ii): "Apps must not write false or inaccurate data into HealthKit ... and may not store personal health information in iCloud." — [Apple App Review Guidelines](https://developer.apple.com/app-store/review/guidelines/)
- 5.1.2(vi): data from HealthKit "may not be used for marketing, advertising or use-based data mining, including by third parties." 2.5.1: "HealthKit should be used for health and fitness purposes and integrate with the Health app." — [Apple App Review Guidelines](https://developer.apple.com/app-store/review/guidelines/)
- 1.4.1: "Medical apps that could provide inaccurate data or information, or that could be used for diagnosing or treating patients may be reviewed with greater scrutiny." "Apps should remind users to check with a doctor in addition to using the app and before making medical decisions." The guidelines contain no rule that names weight, diet or calories. — [Apple App Review Guidelines](https://developer.apple.com/app-store/review/guidelines/)

Metadata, subscriptions, trials
- 2.3.1(a): "marketing your app in a misleading way, such as by promoting content or services that it does not actually offer ... or promoting a false price, whether within or outside of the App Store, is grounds for removal". — [Apple App Review Guidelines](https://developer.apple.com/app-store/review/guidelines/)
- 3.1.2(c): "Before asking a customer to subscribe, you should clearly describe what the user will get for the price." 3.1.2(a): "Apps that attempt to scam users will be removed from the App Store. This includes apps that attempt to trick users into purchasing a subscription under false pretenses or engage in bait-and-switch". 3.1.1 (free trials for non-subscription apps): the app "must clearly identify its duration, the content or services that will no longer be accessible when the trial ends, and any downstream charges". — [Apple App Review Guidelines](https://developer.apple.com/app-store/review/guidelines/)

Children and age ratings
- 5.1.4(a): "Apps may ask for birthdate and parental contact information only for the purpose of complying with these statutes" (COPPA, GDPR and similar). — [Apple App Review Guidelines](https://developer.apple.com/app-store/review/guidelines/)
- Apple replaced the 12+ and 17+ ratings with 13+, 16+ and 18+ in 2025. The questionnaire gained required questions on in-app controls, capabilities, "medical or wellness topics" and violent themes; developers had to answer by 31 January 2026 or updates are blocked. Search summary of Apple's reference page: frequent medical or treatment information sits in the 16+ tier (unverified against the page itself). — [Apple: Updated age ratings](https://developer.apple.com/news/?id=ks775ehf); [Apple age ratings reference](https://developer.apple.com/help/app-store-connect/reference/age-ratings); [MacRumors](https://www.macrumors.com/2025/07/25/apple-overhauls-app-store-age-ratings/)

Enforcement: Cal AI, April 2026
- Apple briefly removed Cal AI (owned by MyFitnessPal since March 2026) in mid-April 2026 and restored it within days after fixes. Per TechCrunch's account of Apple's statements: (1) an embedded Stripe payment flow removed Apple in-app purchase from checkout (3.1.1); (2) the paywall showed the weekly-equivalent price more prominently than the amount billed, and a free-trial toggle obscured auto-renewal information (3.1.2(c)); (3) users who declined the first offer were shown a second, different subscription flow, treated as manipulative under 5.6 (Developer Code of Conduct). — [TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2026/04/21/apples-cal-ai-crackdown-signals-its-still-policing-the-app-store/); [MacRumors](https://www.macrumors.com/2026/04/21/apple-cal-ai-app-store-removal/); [9to5Mac](https://9to5mac.com/2026/04/21/popular-calorie-tracker-briefly-pulled-from-app-store-over-iap-and-billing-violations/)

### Inferences
- Ember's sign-in wall (owner's decision) is defensible under 5.1.1(v) only if review accepts that it has "significant account-based features". Cross-device sync, the coach link, squads and the per-person server cap on AI requests are the features to name in the Notes for Review. The risk of a 5.1.1(v) rejection is real because food search, scanning and manual logging could work without an account.
- Sign in with Apple (4.8) is not required while Ember offers only its own email and password or emailed link. Adding Google or Facebook login would trigger 4.8.
- In-app account deletion is mandatory on day one of the iOS build, and it must be a real deletion path, reachable inside the app.
- Sending a meal photo, voice transcript or chat message to a US AI provider is "third-party AI" sharing under 5.1.2(i). A sentence in the privacy policy is unlikely to be enough; an explicit, in-flow permission naming the AI provider before first AI use is the safer reading. This can be combined with the GDPR explicit consent in section 3.
- Asking for sex, age, height, weight and goal is "directly relevant to the core functionality" of a calorie target, so 5.1.1(v) permits it; asking for anything not used in the calculation (for example a phone number, or a "how did you hear about us" answer forced before use) is harder to defend under 5.1.1(iii).
- Apple's HIG points against requesting notification, camera, microphone or Health permissions during onboarding. Ask for camera at first scan or photo, microphone at first voice use, Health at first wearable connection. A pre-alert screen must have a single "Continue" style button, not "Allow"/"Not now".
- 5.1.1(ix) suggests the Apple developer account should be held by a company, not by the owner as an individual, because the app requires sensitive (health) information.
- The Cal AI case shows which paywall devices Apple currently punishes. Ember is free, so the direct lesson applies only if a paid tier is added later.

### Gaps
- I could not read the last 14,000 characters of the guidelines page (after 5.2.5), so 5.6 (Developer Code of Conduct) wording is taken from press reports only.
- Apple publishes no rule on minimum calorie targets, eating-disorder safeguards or weight-loss projections. I found no Apple statement on these.
- Whether Apple's 2025 to 2026 age-assurance tools (Declared Age Range API) are available or required for UK and Irish users was not confirmed.
- Whether Apple enforces 5.1.2(i) by requiring a named AI provider on the consent screen is reported by secondary sources only.

## 2. Google Play policies

### Takeaway
Google Play requires an in-app and web route to delete the account and its data, a Health apps declaration for every app, an in-app prominent disclosure and consent before collecting health data, and a "not a medical device" statement in the store description for health apps that are not regulated devices. Its subscription policy lists specific deceptive paywall patterns as violations.

### Cited Findings
- Account deletion: "Temporary account deactivation, disabling, or "freezing" the app account does not qualify as account deletion." The developer "must also delete the user data associated with that app account." The option must be discoverable inside the app and through a web link outside it (page read in fragments). — [Google Play User Data policy](https://support.google.com/googleplay/android-developer/answer/10144311?hl=en)
- Personal and sensitive user data expressly includes "health data, Health Connect data". — [Google Play User Data policy](https://support.google.com/googleplay/android-developer/answer/10144311?hl=en)
- Prominent disclosure: it "Must be displayed in the normal usage of the app and not require the user to navigate into a menu or settings" and "Cannot be included with other disclosures unrelated to personal and sensitive user data collection." Consent "Must be granted by the user before your app can begin to collect or access the personal and sensitive user data." "runtime permission requests must be immediately preceded by an in-app disclosure". "Don't request device permissions before users have provided their consent." A privacy policy alone does not satisfy the disclosure duty. — [Google Play User Data policy](https://support.google.com/googleplay/android-developer/answer/10144311?hl=en)
- Health apps declaration: every app on Google Play, including apps with no health features and apps on test tracks, must complete it in Play Console (Policy > App content); required since 31 August 2024. Health Connect questions are part of the same form. — [Google Play: Health apps declaration](https://support.google.com/googleplay/android-developer/answer/13996367?hl=en); [Android: declare access](https://developer.android.com/health-and-fitness/health-connect/declare-access)
- Health Content and Services policy: health apps that are not regulated devices must state in the description that the app is "not a medical device and does not diagnose, treat, cure, or prevent any medical condition." "We don't allow apps with health and medical related functionalities that are misleading or potentially harmful." "Permissions that are not required for a health app to perform its core functionality should not be requested." Health Connect data is "regarded as personal and sensitive user data". Privacy policy must be linked in Play Console and in the app, publicly accessible, not geofenced and not a PDF. — [Google Play: Health Content and Services](https://support.google.com/googleplay/android-developer/answer/16679511?hl=en)
- Subscriptions: "You must be transparent about your offer." Trials: "Be sure to let your users know how and when a free trial will convert to a paid subscription". Apps must give "access to an easy-to-use, online method to cancel the subscription." Listed violations include "Annual subscriptions that most prominently display their pricing in terms of monthly cost", "Multiple screens in the purchase flow that lead users into accidentally clicking the subscribe button", and a missing or unclear dismiss button. "We do not allow apps that subject users to deceptive or manipulative purchase experiences." — [Google Play: Subscriptions policy](https://support.google.com/googleplay/android-developer/answer/9900533?hl=en)
- Target audience: developers declare age groups in Play Console; misdeclaration can lead to removal. Apps with a mixed audience need a "neutral age screen" that "doesn't encourage them to falsify their age"; pre-setting the birth date to a qualifying age is an incorrect set-up. Imagery and wording that appeals to children can override the declared audience. — [Google Play: Target audience and content](https://support.google.com/googleplay/android-developer/answer/9867159?hl=en); [Google Play Families policy](https://support.google.com/googleplay/android-developer/answer/9893335?hl=en)

### Inferences
- The Android build needs: in-app delete account, a public web page for deletion requests, the Data safety form's deletion section, the Health apps declaration, and the "not a medical device" sentence in the listing.
- Google's prominent-disclosure rule and GDPR explicit consent point to the same design: a dedicated screen about health data, separate from the terms of service, shown before the profile questions, with an affirmative action.
- If Ember declares an adults-only audience, the age question must be neutral (an empty date or year field with no default and no hint of the cut-off).

### Gaps
- The Health Connect policy's list of approved use cases (fitness and wellness and so on) was not retrieved in full; check before wearable work.
- I found no Google Play policy text on minimum calorie targets or eating-disorder safeguards. Whether Google's content policies name pro-eating-disorder content was not confirmed.
- Google policy pages change often; the texts above were read on 26 November 2026.

## 3. Data protection: UK GDPR, EU GDPR, children, transfers, DPIA, regulators

### Takeaway
Treat Ember's profile, weight, food diary, training and wearable data as special category health data and collect explicit, separate, unticked, withdrawable consent before the profile questions, naming the US AI provider. Ireland's digital age of consent is 16 and the UK's is 13; an 18+ service still falls under the UK Children's Code if children are likely to use it. A DPIA is needed before launch.

### Cited Findings
Is this health data?
- UK GDPR Article 4(15) health data is "personal data related to the physical or mental health of a natural person" and covers "past, current or future health status." — [ICO: What is special category data?](https://ico.org.uk/for-organisations/uk-gdpr-guidance-and-resources/lawful-basis/special-category-data/what-is-special-category-data/)
- ICO on wearables: "Information from body-worn devices, such as fitness trackers, isn't necessarily health data." Step counts alone do not reveal health status, but it can become health data: "This might be if a heart rate is recorded, or if a device records BMI scores." — [ICO: What is special category data?](https://ico.org.uk/for-organisations/uk-gdpr-guidance-and-resources/lawful-basis/special-category-data/what-is-special-category-data/)
- ICO on inferences: "the inferred data is special category data regardless of how confident you are that the inference is correct." — [ICO: What is special category data?](https://ico.org.uk/for-organisations/uk-gdpr-guidance-and-resources/lawful-basis/special-category-data/what-is-special-category-data/)
- EU position: in Lindenapotheke (C-21/23, 4 October 2024) the Court of Justice held that data on online orders of non-prescription pharmacy medicines are health data because health status can be revealed "by means of an intellectual operation involving comparison or deduction"; it rejected the Advocate General's narrower view. Commentators read this as confirming a broad, probability-based test. — [CJEU press release](https://curia.europa.eu/jcms/upload/docs/application/pdf/2024-10/cp240159en.pdf); [Bird & Bird](https://cm.twobirds.com/en/insights/2024/global/feeling-unwell-after-the-cjeus-lindenapotheke-decision); [Taylor Wessing](https://www.taylorwessing.com/en/insights-and-events/insights/2024/10/ecj-lindenapotheke)

Explicit consent (Article 9(2)(a))
- Explicit consent "must be confirmed by the person in a clear statement", "must specify the nature of the special category data", and "should ensure explicit consent is separate from any other consents you're seeking." — [ICO: conditions for processing](https://ico.org.uk/for-organisations/uk-gdpr-guidance-and-resources/lawful-basis/special-category-data/what-are-the-conditions-for-processing/)
- Explicit consent can be valid "even if it's a condition of service", but the controller "must demonstrate consent is still freely given" and the processing must be "objectively necessary to perform the contractual service." — [ICO: conditions for processing](https://ico.org.uk/for-organisations/uk-gdpr-guidance-and-resources/lawful-basis/special-category-data/what-are-the-conditions-for-processing/)
- Valid consent: not "a precondition of a service, but the processing is not necessary for that service"; not "bundled up with other terms and conditions"; "granular consent options for different types of processing"; "you need to identify yourself, and also name any third party controllers who will be relying on the consent"; "Silence, pre-ticked boxes or inactivity should not therefore constitute consent"; "Explicit consent must be expressly confirmed in words"; withdrawable "easily at any time"; records must be kept; "The UK GDPR does not set a specific time limit for consent." — [ICO: What is valid consent?](https://ico.org.uk/for-organisations/uk-gdpr-guidance-and-resources/lawful-basis/consent/what-is-valid-consent/)

ICO review of health-adjacent apps
- The ICO reviewed period and fertility apps in 2023 and reported in February 2024 that it found no serious compliance issues or evidence of harm, but reminded all developers of apps "that support our health and wellbeing" of four duties: "Be transparent", "Obtain valid consent", "Establish the correct lawful basis", "Be accountable". It said consent must be "explicit, unambiguous and involve a clear action to opt-in" and "You must not use pre-ticked boxes or any default method for consent." "You must not adopt a one-size-fits-all approach." — [ICO, 8 February 2024](https://ico.org.uk/about-the-ico/media-centre/news-and-blogs/2024/02/ico-urges-all-app-developers-to-prioritise-privacy)

Children
- UK: "the processing of the personal data of a child shall be lawful where the child is at least 13 years old"; under 13 needs consent from the holder of parental responsibility, with "reasonable efforts, taking into consideration available technology, to verify". Article 8 bites only where consent is the lawful basis. Low-risk processing may need only self-declaration; higher-risk services may need third-party verification. "Collecting excessive information is unlikely to comply with the data protection by design approach." — [ICO: ISS and consent](https://ico.org.uk/for-organisations/uk-gdpr-guidance-and-resources/childrens-information/children-and-the-uk-gdpr/what-are-the-rules-about-an-iss-and-consent/)
- Ireland: the age of digital consent is 16; below that, consent-based processing needs a parent or guardian, with "reasonable efforts" to verify. The DPC's Fundamentals ("Children Front and Centre", December 2021) set 14 principles including a "floor of protection": services should give all users a child-appropriate floor "unless they take a risk-based approach to verifying the age of their users". — [DPC: Fundamentals (PDF)](https://www.dataprotection.ie/sites/default/files/uploads/2021-12/Fundamentals%20for%20a%20Child-Oriented%20Approach%20to%20Data%20Processing_FINAL_EN.pdf); [DPC blog](https://www.dataprotection.ie/en/dpc-guidance/blogs/the-children-fundamentals); [Covington summary](https://www.insideprivacy.com/childrens-privacy/dpc-publishes-guidance-on-processing-childrens-personal-data/)
- UK Children's Code (Age Appropriate Design Code): 15 standards for services "likely to be accessed by children" (under 18). Since September 2022 the ICO's position is that adult-only services are in scope if children are likely to access them; "likely" means more probable than not; a provider that concludes it is out of scope should document its reasons. — [ICO: likely to be accessed by children](https://ico.org.uk/for-organisations/uk-gdpr-guidance-and-resources/childrens-information/childrens-code-guidance-and-resources/likely-to-be-accessed-by-children/); [ICO: Age appropriate design code](https://ico.org.uk/for-organisations/guide-to-data-protection/ico-codes-of-practice/age-appropriate-design-code)

International transfers to a US AI provider
- EU: the EU-US Data Privacy Framework adequacy decision was upheld by the General Court on 3 September 2025 (Latombe v Commission). An appeal to the Court of Justice was lodged on 31 October 2025 (C-703/25 P). I found no report of a hearing or judgment as of November 2026. — [IAPP](https://iapp.org/news/a/european-general-court-dismisses-latombe-challenge-upholds-eu-us-data-privacy-framework); [WilmerHale](https://www.wilmerhale.com/en/insights/blogs/wilmerhale-privacy-and-cybersecurity-law/20251201-european-court-of-justice-to-review-challenge-to-eu-us-data-privacy-framework); [EUR-Lex notice of appeal](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/C/2025/6610/oj/eng)
- UK: the UK-US data bridge took effect on 12 October 2023. It works only for US recipients certified to the DPF that have also opted into the UK Extension. The ICO's opinion flagged weaker definitions of "sensitive" data among other concerns. Practitioners advise keeping the UK IDTA or Addendum as a fallback. — [Pinsent Masons](https://www.pinsentmasons.com/out-law/news/uk-us-data-bridge-take-effect-12-october); [Slaughter and May](https://thelens.slaughterandmay.com/post/102iodz/us-adequacy-by-another-name-uk-us-data-bridge-process-concludes); [Kennedys on ICO 2026 transfer guidance](https://www.kennedyslaw.com/en/thought-leadership/article/2026/the-ico-s-2026-updated-international-transfer-guidance-decoding-the-new-uk-regime)
- Anthropic's Data Processing Addendum is incorporated into its Commercial Terms and includes the EU Standard Contractual Clauses (Module Two or Three) and the UK Addendum. — [Anthropic DPA](https://www.anthropic.com/legal/data-processing-addendum); [Anthropic support](https://support.claude.com/en/articles/7996862-how-do-i-view-and-sign-your-data-processing-addendum-dpa)

DPIA
- ICO: a DPIA is required for large-scale special category data and for "new technologies, or the novel application of existing technologies (including AI)" combined with another risk factor; its examples include "hardware/software offering fitness/lifestyle/health monitoring". — [ICO: examples of high-risk processing](https://ico.org.uk/for-organisations/uk-gdpr-guidance-and-resources/accountability-and-governance/data-protection-impact-assessments-dpias/examples-of-processing-likely-to-result-in-high-risk); [ICO: when do we need a DPIA](https://ico.org.uk/for-organisations/uk-gdpr-guidance-and-resources/accountability-and-governance/data-protection-impact-assessments-dpias/when-do-we-need-to-do-a-dpia)
- DPC: its Article 35(4) list includes special category data used with profiling or algorithmic means, systematic monitoring or tracking of behaviour, and innovative technology; meeting two criteria generally means a DPIA; "where there is a doubt, conducting a DPIA is advised". — [DPC: DPIA page](https://www.dataprotection.ie/en/organisations/know-your-obligations/data-protection-impact-assessments); [DPC list (PDF)](https://www.dataprotection.ie/sites/default/files/uploads/2018-11/Data-Protection-Impact-Assessment.pdf)

UK Data (Use and Access) Act 2025
- Most data protection provisions commenced on 5 February 2026; the ICO marked full commencement on 19 June 2026 (including the new right to complain to the controller). PECR maximum fines rose from £500,000 to UK GDPR levels (£17.5 million or 4% of global turnover). The new soft opt-in is for charities only. — [gov.uk commencement plans](https://www.gov.uk/guidance/data-use-and-access-act-2025-plans-for-commencement); [ICO, June 2026](https://ico.org.uk/about-the-ico/media-centre/news-and-blogs/2026/06/one-year-on-marking-the-12-month-commencement-of-the-data-use-and-access-act/); [DLA Piper](https://privacymatters.dlapiper.com/2026/02/uk-commencement-of-the-data-protection-provisions-in-the-data-use-and-access-act/); [Clifford Chance](https://www.cliffordchance.com/insights/resources/blogs/talking-tech/en/articles/2026/02/key-aspects-of-the-data--use-and-access--act-take-effect.html)

### Inferences
- Under the ICO's own examples (BMI, heart rate) and the CJEU's deduction test, Ember's data is health data: height and weight give BMI, a weight-loss goal plus a food diary reveals weight management, wearables add heart rate and sleep. Design on the basis that Article 9 applies to the whole profile and diary. Do not rely on the "step counts are not health data" example.
- Consent screen design that follows from the sources: shown before the first health question; its own screen, not inside the terms; states in words what is collected (for example "your weight, height, age, sex, food diary, training and any wearable data you connect") and why; a button or unticked box whose label is itself the statement ("I agree to Ember using my health information to ..."); a separate, optional consent for AI features that names the US provider and says photos, voice transcripts and chat text are sent there; a separate optional consent for each coach share; a settings page to withdraw each one, with a plain statement of what stops working.
- Because calorie targets cannot be calculated without the profile, consent to the core processing can be required to use the app (ICO: valid "even if it's a condition of service" where "objectively necessary"). AI features, coach sharing, wearables and marketing are not objectively necessary for manual logging, so each needs its own opt-in and the app must work without them. Ember already runs without AI, which supports this split.
- Data minimisation: each onboarding question should feed a calculation or a safeguard. Sex, age, height, weight, activity and goal do. Questions used only for persuasion or marketing segmentation (a common pattern in competitor funnels) do not.
- Privacy information belongs on the screen where the data is asked for (a one-line "why we ask" under weight, for example) with a link to the full notice. The ICO's "Be transparent" tip names purposes, retention and recipients.
- Age: an 18+ rule (matching MyFitnessPal and Noom, section 7) removes the Ireland 16 / UK 13 split and aligns with the advertising rule against weight-control marketing to under-18s (section 5). It does not by itself take Ember outside the Children's Code or the DPC Fundamentals: a self-declared date of birth is the minimum, and whether stronger age assurance is proportionate for health data is a judgement to document in the DPIA.
- Transfers: for an Irish controller the lawful routes to a US AI provider are the DPF (if the provider is certified) or SCCs, which the Anthropic DPA already contains. The pending CJEU appeal makes SCCs plus a transfer impact assessment the prudent base rather than DPF alone. For UK users, the UK Addendum in the same DPA covers the transfer. The project's own rule (no health data outside the EU without asking the owner) means this is the owner's decision.
- A DPIA is required before launch on both regulators' lists: health data, AI processing, tracking of behaviour, possible minors, and a third-country processor.

### Gaps
- Unsettled: no ICO or DPC decision says in terms that a calorie or weight diary is always health data. The ICO's examples and Lindenapotheke make it the safe assumption; a specialist should confirm the lawful-basis design (explicit consent for everything versus contract plus explicit consent).
- I did not retrieve the EDPB or Article 29 Working Party material on health data in lifestyle apps, nor EDPB Guidelines 03/2022 on deceptive design patterns.
- Whether Anthropic is currently on the DPF list with the UK Extension was not verified (only a third-party document suggests so). Check the DPF list directly.
- The outcome of the UK government's 2026 review of data bridges was not found.
- The outcome of Ireland's statutory review of the age of digital consent was not found; 16 is stated in DPC material from 2021 to 2022.
- DUAA provisions on children (new duties for services likely to be accessed by children) were not verified in this session.
- Not researched: whether an Irish company with UK users needs a UK representative under Article 27 UK GDPR, and ICO registration or fee questions. Ask a specialist.
- I found no ICO or DPC enforcement decision against a calorie-tracking or fitness app. The ICO period-app review ended in guidance, not enforcement.

## 4. Consumer law and dark patterns: UK, Ireland, EU

### Takeaway
In the UK, false urgency, fake reviews and drip pricing are banned outright under the DMCC Act (in force 6 April 2025), and the CMA has been fining directly since late 2025. In Ireland the same ground is covered by the Consumer Rights Act 2022 and the EU Unfair Commercial Practices Directive; the EU's Digital Fairness Act was proposed on 11 November 2026 and is not law. Onboarding devices that state or imply something untrue (a fake "calculating" delay, invented user counts, a timer that resets) are the main exposure.

### Cited Findings
UK: DMCC Act 2024, unfair commercial practices
- The unfair commercial practices provisions "apply to commercial practices which happen from 6 April 2025 onwards." CMA guidance CMA207 (updated 18 November 2025) covers 32 practices banned in all circumstances (Schedule 20), regardless of effect on the average consumer. — [CMA207 on gov.uk](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/unfair-commercial-practices-cma207/unfair-commercial-practices); [CMA207 PDF](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/691b9bd821ef5aaa6543ee6f/Unfair_commercial_practices_CMA207_18_Nov_2025__2_.pdf)
- Banned practices relevant here (CMA numbering): 7 "Falsely stating that a product will only be available for a limited time", with the CMA's example of a trader who "falsely tells a consumer that an offer will end when a countdown clock runs out"; 3 displaying a trust mark without authorisation; 19 "Falsely claiming that a product can" ... "prevent or treat disease"; 23 "Describing a product as 'gratis', 'free', 'without charge' or similar" when it is not; 28 "Making persistent and unwanted solicitations by any means". Fake reviews (Schedule 20 paragraph 13): "Submitting, or commissioning another person to submit or write" ... "a fake consumer review" or one that conceals an incentive. — [CMA207 on gov.uk](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/unfair-commercial-practices-cma207/unfair-commercial-practices); [CMA208 fake reviews guidance](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/67eeb64fe9c76fa33048c790/CMA208_-_Fake_reviews_guidance.pdf)
- CMA examples: a pop-up "Be quick! We've sold 10 in the last 5 mins." can mislead if untrue; a cancellation journey that "requires customers to click multiple times" with unnecessary warnings is an aggressive practice. — [CMA207 on gov.uk](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/unfair-commercial-practices-cma207/unfair-commercial-practices)
- Drip pricing (s.230): the total price including mandatory charges must be in the invitation to purchase. — [CMA207 on gov.uk](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/unfair-commercial-practices-cma207/unfair-commercial-practices)
- Vulnerable consumers: those "vulnerable because of age, physical or mental health, credulity or the circumstances they are in"; the test is whether the trader could reasonably have been expected to foresee the vulnerability. — [CMA207 on gov.uk](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/unfair-commercial-practices-cma207/unfair-commercial-practices)
- CMA direct enforcement: fines up to 10% of global turnover (or £300,000 if higher), and up to £300,000 for individuals, without going to court (law-firm summaries). First outcomes: Euro Car Parks about £500,000 (December 2025, failure to answer an information request); the AA and BSM driving schools £4.2 million plus refunds to over 80,000 customers (April 2026, drip pricing); Marks Electrical £720,000 (June 2026, automatic opt-in to add-ons); StubHub close to £900,000 (June 2026, drip pricing). By autumn 2026: 19 businesses investigated and over 100 advisory letters. CMA priorities for 2026 to 2027: drip pricing, fake reviews, pressure selling. — [Osborne Clarke](https://www.osborneclarke.com/insights/cma-issues-first-fines-under-its-new-uk-direct-consumer-enforcement-powers); [Mayer Brown, August 2026](https://www.mayerbrown.com/en/insights/publications/2026/08/were-just-getting-started-the-cmas-new-consumer-enforcement-powers-in-action); [Cooley, October 2026](https://www.cooley.com/news/insight/2026/2026-10-08-uk-consumer-protection-regime); [DLA Piper](https://www.dlapiper.com/insights/publications/2026/04/the-aa-agrees-a-fine-in-the-first-cma-consumer-protection-enforcement-under-the-dmcca)
- Urgency cases under the earlier law: Emma Sleep admitted breaking consumer law with misleading countdown timers, false "high demand" messages and discount claims, in a court-endorsed settlement; Wowcher gave undertakings on timers and refunded over £4 million. — [CMA: Emma Sleep](https://www.gov.uk/government/news/court-endorses-cma-action-as-emma-sleep-agrees-to-change-sales-practices); [CMA: Wowcher](https://gov.uk/government/news/cma-secures-over-4-million-in-refunds-for-wowcher-customers); [CMA: urgency claims programme](https://www.gov.uk/government/news/cma-investigates-online-selling-practices-based-on-urgency-claims)

UK: DMCC subscription contracts regime (not yet in force)
- Sources conflict on the date. Lewis Silkin (June 2026) and Reed Smith (April 2026): delayed to spring 2027. DWF (22 October 2026): the "Government has confirmed that the regime will come into force in January 2027". Ireland
- The Consumer Rights Act 2022 commenced on 29 November 2022. It prohibits posting fake reviews and misleading search rankings, and requires traders who show reviews to take "reasonable and proportionate" steps to verify them; the CCPC can bring enforcement leading to court-imposed fines. — [CCPC](https://www.ccpc.ie/news-and-media/news/article/2022/11/28/ccpc-welcomes-new-rights-for-consumers); [Law Society Gazette](https://www.lawsociety.ie/gazette/top-stories/2022/november/beefed-up-consumer-rights-law-comes-into-force/); [Mason Hayes & Curran](https://www.mhc.ie/latest/insights/country-update-ireland-the-consumer-rights-act-2022)
- The CCPC has said dark patterns can fall under the Unfair Commercial Practices Directive but "the bar to prove a dark pattern under the UCPD is high", and in 2025 that the law lacks clarity on what a dark pattern is. — [CCPC submission, 2023](https://www.ccpc.ie/business/wp-content/uploads/sites/3/2023/03/2023.02.20-IE-CCPC-Submission-on-Digital-Fairness-Fitness-Check.pdf); [CCPC submission, October 2025](https://www.ccpc.ie/business/wp-content/uploads/sites/3/2025/12/2025.10.24-CCPC-Submission-to-EC-Digital-Fairness-Act-Consultation.pdf)

EU
- The Commission's UCPD guidance (Notice of 17 December 2021) has a section (4.2.7) on data-driven practices and dark patterns; dark patterns can be misleading (Articles 6 and 7) or aggressive (Articles 8 and 9) practices. Commission material gives "fake countdown timers" as a clear case. The Notice is not binding. — [European Commission: UCPD](https://commission.europa.eu/law/law-topic/consumer-protection-law/unfair-commercial-practices-law/unfair-commercial-practices-directive_en); [Covington](https://www.insideprivacy.com/eu-data-protection/the-eu-stance-on-dark-patterns/); [Osborne Clarke](https://www.osborneclarke.com/insights/digital-fairness-act-unpacked-dark-patterns)
- Consumer Rights Directive Article 22 requires express consent, without pre-selection, for any extra payment. — [Osborne Clarke](https://www.osborneclarke.com/insights/digital-fairness-act-unpacked-dark-patterns)
- Digital Services Act: Article 25 bans deceptive interface design by online platforms, but does not apply to practices already covered by the UCPD or GDPR, and Article 19 excludes micro and small enterprises (under 50 staff and under €10 million turnover) from the online-platform section. — [DSA Article 19 text](https://www.springlex.eu/en/packages/dsa/dsa-regulation/article-19/); [Osborne Clarke](https://www.osborneclarke.com/insights/digital-fairness-act-unpacked-dark-patterns)
- Digital Fairness Act: proposed by the Commission on 11 November 2026 as a regulation replacing the UCPD (reported reference COM(2026) 1100, procedure 2026/0356(COD); reference not verified against the Commission text). Per a law-firm summary of the proposal: Article 10 bans manipulative design, with ten practices banned outright, including false availability or popularity claims, persistent scarcity messages, default add-ons the consumer must reject, framing the decline option as the wrong choice, repeatedly re-asking for a choice already made, ambiguous wording, giving less prominence to price or renewal terms, false notifications, and cancellation made disproportionately hard. Article 17 (addictive design, not limited to minors) would require easy tools to switch off autoplay, notifications unrelated to the user's activity, "features encouraging engagement at regular times, including through penalties", and simulated personal relationships from automated systems. Article 20: free trials need a reminder and express acceptance before conversion. Article 23: a "terminate the contract here" function. Fines up to 6% of worldwide turnover for EU-wide infringements. Application 12 months after entry into force; it must first pass Parliament and Council. — [Cuatrecasas](https://www.cuatrecasas.com/en/latam/intellectual-property/art/european-commission-digital-fairness-act); [European Parliament legislative train](https://www.europarl.europa.eu/legislative-train/theme-protecting-our-democracy-upholding-our-values/file-digital-fairness-act); [IAPP](https://iapp.org/news/a/european-commission-releases-draft-proposal-for-digital-fairness-act)

### Inferences
Risk reading of specific onboarding devices (my analysis, applying the rules above):
- Fake progress or "calculating your plan" screens: a short animation is not itself banned. It becomes a misleading action if it claims analysis that is not happening (for example "analysing 12,000 similar users" when the target is a formula). Ember's own rule (nothing fake presented as real) already excludes this. A truthful label such as "Working out your daily target" is low risk.
- Fabricated social proof (invented user counts, ratings, testimonials, "people like you lost X"): banned as fake reviews in the UK (Schedule 20 paragraph 13) and under the Irish 2022 Act, and a misleading action in any case. Real testimonials about weight loss carry the advertising rules in section 5.
- Countdown timers and "offer ends" messages: banned practice 7 if untrue; the Emma Sleep and Wowcher cases are the reference points. A timer that restarts for each visitor is the classic breach.
- Confirmshaming ("No thanks, I don't care about my health"): not named in CMA207, so it is judged under the general aggressive or misleading tests in the UK today; it would be banned outright under DFA Article 10 as proposed. In a health app aimed at people who may be vulnerable about weight it is a poor risk.
- Pre-ticked boxes: invalid for GDPR consent (section 3) and for marketing (section 9); for paid add-ons, barred by the Consumer Rights Directive and penalised by the CMA (Marks Electrical).
- Hard-to-cancel trials and paywalls after a long questionnaire: not relevant while Ember is free. If a paid tier is added, the UK subscription regime (January or spring 2027), DFA Article 20 and 23 if adopted, and both app stores' rules all apply. A long questionnaire that ends in an undisclosed paywall also risks being a misleading omission.
- Projected weight-loss dates: see section 5.
- Streaks and XP: lawful today. DFA Article 17 as proposed would require an easy way to switch off features that encourage engagement at regular times "including through penalties", which describes a streak that is lost on a missed day. Ember's existing "break that holds the streak" and a setting to turn streaks and reminders off would anticipate this.
- "Free": Ember can describe itself as free only while use carries no unavoidable cost (banned practice 23). A daily AI cap is a limit, not a cost, but should be disclosed where "free" is claimed.
- The DSA is unlikely to apply to Ember: it is a micro or small enterprise and probably not an "online platform".

### Gaps
- The DMCC subscription regime start date is unconfirmed by a primary government source in my research (January 2027 versus spring 2027). Check gov.uk before relying on either.
- I could not read section 4.2.7 of the UCPD Notice directly, so I cannot confirm whether it names confirmshaming or free-trial traps.
- CMA fining thresholds are taken from law-firm summaries, not from the Act or the CMA's penalties guidance.
- The DFA text was read only through one law-firm summary; article numbers and content may change in the legislative process, and SME treatment is unknown.
- No regulator decision in the UK or Ireland was found on "calculating" screens or onboarding questionnaires in health apps. This area is untested.

## 5. Advertising and health claims: ASA/CAP, ASAI, CCPC, EU claims rules

### Takeaway
UK CAP Code section 13 bars claims that people can lose precise amounts of weight in a stated period, bars weight-reduction marketing aimed at under-18s or at anyone who would end up below BMI 18.5, and treats more than 2 lb (about 1 kg) a week as incompatible with good practice for people who are overweight. Ireland's ASAI code has matching slimming rules. Whether an in-app "you will reach X kg by date" screen is an advertisement is not settled, but the same statement in a store listing, website or social ad plainly is.

### Cited Findings
- CAP Code 13.9: "Marketing communications must not contain claims that people can lose precise amounts of weight within a stated period." — [CAP Code section 13](https://www.asa.org.uk/type/non_broadcast/code_section/13.html)
- 13.3: "Marketing communications for any weight-reduction regime or establishment must neither be directed at nor contain anything" likely to appeal particularly to "people who are under 18" or those for whom weight reduction would produce "a potentially harmful body weight (BMI of less than 18.5 kg/m2)". Marketing must not suggest that being underweight is desirable. — [CAP Code section 13](https://www.asa.org.uk/type/non_broadcast/code_section/13.html)
- 13.10: "Claims that an individual has lost an exact amount of weight must be compatible with good medical and nutritional practice", must state the period, and must not be based on unrepresentative experiences; for those who are normally overweight, a loss of "greater than 2 lbs (just under 1 kg) a week" is unlikely to be compatible. — [CAP Code section 13](https://www.asa.org.uk/type/non_broadcast/code_section/13.html)
- 13.1: "Any claim made for the effectiveness or action of a weight-reduction method or product must be backed" by evidence; "testimonials that are not supported by trials do not constitute substantiation." 13.5: "Marketers must be able to show that their diet plans are nutritionally well-balanced". 13.7: diets "that fall below 800 kilo-calories a day" must be short term "and must encourage users to take medical advice before embarking on them." 13.11: "Marketing communications for intensive exercise programmes should encourage users to check with a doctor before starting." — [CAP Code section 13](https://www.asa.org.uk/type/non_broadcast/code_section/13.html)
- 13.2 and 13.2.1: obesity (BMI over 30) treatments may not be advertised to the public unless under suitably qualified supervision; lifestyle programmes may make "responsible references to obesity" only if they are "shown to be effective at 12 months or beyond", "last at least three months", are "multi-component", and are "developed by a multi-disciplinary team; including input from a registered dietician". — [CAP Code section 13](https://www.asa.org.uk/type/non_broadcast/code_section/13.html)
- CAP AdviceOnline (page dated 5 May 2026): avoid claims of "a precise amount of weight within a stated period of time"; marketers should not imply dieters cannot fail or that loss is permanent; before-and-after photos are treated as testimonials; marketers "should not exploit the vulnerable." The page does not mention apps or personalised predictions. — [CAP: Weight control: General](https://www.asa.org.uk/advice-online/weight-control-general.html)
- ASA has applied the weight rules to ads served inside apps: an in-app ad claiming "LOSE 4 STONE IN 15 DAYS" for a supplement was upheld as a prohibited rate-and-amount claim for a food (rules 15.6, 15.6.6) and as irresponsible (1.3). CAP rule 13.10.1: "Health claims in marketing communications for food products that refer to a rate or amount of weight loss are not permitted." — [ASA ruling: Premium Health Europe BV](https://asa.org.uk/rulings/premium-health-europe-bv-a22-1176133-premium-health-europe-bv.html); [CMS summary](https://cms.law/en/gbr/legal-updates/big-facts-no-figures-weight-management-food-supplement-ad-gets-a-grilling-from-the-asa)
- Recent ASA weight-loss enforcement (2024 to 2026) has concentrated on ads for prescription-only weight-loss medicines and supplements (for example Chequp Health, Juniper, MedExpress). — [ASA rulings: Chequp Health](https://www.asa.org.uk/rulings/chequp-health-ltd--a25-1305722-chequp-health-ltd-.html); [ASA rulings: Juniper](https://www.asa.org.uk/rulings/juniper-technologies-uk-ltd-a26-1341966-juniper-technologies-uk-ltd.html)
- The ASA has published advice on when health app marketing claims turn software into a medical device. — [ASA: A pulse check for health apps](https://www.asa.org.uk/news/a-pulse-check-for-health-apps-when-does-software-become-a-medical-device.html)
- Ireland, ASAI Code slimming section (search extract; page could not be opened): applies to "weight control and slimming foodstuffs, aids ... clinics and other establishments, diets"; effectiveness claims "should be backed by reputable and robust practical trials on human subjects"; "weight loss greater than 1 kilogram (2.2lb) per week in those who are overweight would not be considered compatible with good medical or nutritional practice." The ASAI upheld in part a 7th Edition slimming complaint in August 2025 for lack of evidence. — [ASAI Code: Slimming](https://asai.ie/asai-code/slimming); [ASAI complaints bulletin](https://asai.ie/press-releases/complaints-bulletin-165)
- Ireland, influencers: joint CCPC and ASAI guidance (October 2023) requires commercial content to be labelled; breaches of consumer law can lead to CCPC enforcement and breaches of the ASAI Code to a published adjudication naming the influencer and the brand. — [CCPC/ASAI guidance (PDF)](https://www.ccpc.ie/business/wp-content/uploads/sites/3/2023/10/CCPC-ASAI-Guidance-on-Influencer-Advertising.pdf)

### Inferences
- A projection screen ("At this rate you will reach 78 kg by 14 March") is a statement of a precise amount within a stated period. If it appears in an ad, store screenshot, website or influencer post, CAP 13.9 applies directly and it should not be used. Inside the app after sign-up, it is arguably product functionality, not marketing; but in a free app whose onboarding is designed to convert a visitor into a user, a regulator could treat it as a marketing claim, and it is in any case a commercial practice that must not mislead under the DMCC Act and the UCPD.
- Safer wording that follows from 13.9, 13.10 and the CAP advice: present it as arithmetic, not a promise ("Estimate. A deficit of about 500 kcal a day is roughly 0.5 kg a week. Actual results vary."), show a range or a rate, not a single date; cap any goal pace at 1 kg a week (0.5 to 1 kg is the conventional range); never show it for a goal that would take BMI below 18.5; label it Estimate, which Ember's own rules already require.
- App Store and Play screenshots are ads. A screenshot of the projection screen with a specific kilo and date figure is a 13.9 risk even if the in-app screen is defensible.
- Under 13.3, marketing (including look and tone) should not appeal particularly to under-18s; with an 18+ rule the store age rating and the ad targeting should match.
- Testimonials with exact weight lost need the period, must be typical, and must be within about 1 kg a week. Ember's "no invented reviews" rule covers the fake-review ban; these rules cover real ones.
- Ember should avoid any reference to treating obesity: it does not meet 13.2.1.
- EU nutrition and health claims rules bite on foods, not on an app, so they matter only if Ember promotes food products.

### Gaps
- I found no ASA or ASAI ruling on a calorie-tracking or fasting app's own ads, on a personalised "reach your goal by" claim, or on influencer ads for such apps. This is a real gap, not a search artefact I can rule out: the ASA rulings database should be searched by a specialist before relying on the inferences above.
- Whether in-app onboarding content is within the CAP Code's remit (marketers' own online space) was not confirmed from the Code's scope rules. Unsettled; ask CAP's free Copy Advice service.
- ASAI section and rule numbers are unverified (the site shows inconsistent numbering, and the page returned an access error).

## 6. Medical device boundary: MHRA and EU MDR

### Takeaway
A calorie and training tracker for general fitness and wellbeing is not a medical device in the UK or EU, provided it makes no claim to diagnose, monitor, treat or prevent a disease or injury. What the maker says, in the app, the store listing and ads, decides the question, and a disclaimer does not cure a medical claim made elsewhere.

### Cited Findings
- MHRA guidance (v1.10g, last revised October 2026 for a contact detail): "The monitoring of general fitness, general health and general wellbeing is not usually considered to be a medical purpose". — [MHRA: Medical device stand-alone software including apps (PDF)](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/6abf6c8c943e890cb55e5e52/Medical_device_stand-alone_software_including_apps__including_IVDMDs.pdf); [gov.uk landing page](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/medical-devices-software-applications-apps)
- "Apps and software for monitoring sport or fitness purposes, e.g. heart rate, are not considered to be medical devices. However, in some specific cases, where the intention is to investigate the physiological processes they may be." — [MHRA guidance (PDF)](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/6abf6c8c943e890cb55e5e52/Medical_device_stand-alone_software_including_apps__including_IVDMDs.pdf)
- Unlikely to be devices: apps "that are intended to just provide tips or advice or link to support groups"; software that "offers only lifestyle treatment choices or referral advice (e.g. see your GP)"; apps that "simply replace a written diary/log of symptoms". — [MHRA guidance (PDF)](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/6abf6c8c943e890cb55e5e52/Medical_device_stand-alone_software_including_apps__including_IVDMDs.pdf)
- Medical purposes in the flow chart: prevention, diagnosis, monitoring, treatment or alleviation of disease; diagnosis, monitoring, treatment, alleviation of or compensation for an injury or handicap. "A medical purpose is determined by what the manufacturer states in the device's labelling, instructions for use and any promotional materials." — [MHRA guidance (PDF)](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/6abf6c8c943e890cb55e5e52/Medical_device_stand-alone_software_including_apps__including_IVDMDs.pdf)
- Disclaimers: "General disclaimers (for example 'this product is not a medical device') are not acceptable if medical claims are made or implied elsewhere in the product labelling or associated promotional literature." "Anecdotal quotes and testimonials are considered to be implied claims by the manufacturer if they are repeated in product literature." "Simple changes to the description make the difference between a product being considered a device or not." — [MHRA guidance (PDF)](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/6abf6c8c943e890cb55e5e52/Medical_device_stand-alone_software_including_apps__including_IVDMDs.pdf)
- Indicative words that point towards a device include "Calculates...", "Can benefit those who suffer from…", "Controls…", "Cure/cures…", "Clinically proven...". Decision support "is usually considered a medical device when it applies automated reasoning such as a simple calculation, an algorithm" to healthcare decisions, and software is most likely a device if "It is intended to influence the actual treatment". — [MHRA guidance (PDF)](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/6abf6c8c943e890cb55e5e52/Medical_device_stand-alone_software_including_apps__including_IVDMDs.pdf)
- EU: MDCG 2019-11 was revised on 17 June 2025 (Rev.1), adding clarification on wellness and lifestyle apps and borderline cases; qualification still turns on the manufacturer's intended purpose. A December 2025 Commission draft would amend MDR Rule 11; not in force. — [European Commission: MDCG 2019-11 rev.1](https://health.ec.europa.eu/latest-updates/update-mdcg-2019-11-rev1-qualification-and-classification-software-regulation-eu-2017745-and-2025-06-17_en); [Emergo](https://www.emergobyul.com/news/european-revision-primary-software-guidance-mdcg-2019-11-revision-1-small-changes-meaningful)
- Google Play requires non-device health apps to state they are "not a medical device and does not diagnose, treat, cure, or prevent any medical condition." — [Google Play: Health Content and Services](https://support.google.com/googleplay/android-developer/answer/16679511?hl=en)

### Inferences
- Wording that keeps Ember on the wellness side: "general fitness and wellbeing", "track what you eat and how you train", "estimates", "general guidance". Wording to avoid in the app, listing and ads: treat, manage or reverse a condition (diabetes, obesity, hypertension, PCOS, eating disorders); "clinically proven"; "diagnose"; "prescribed" calories; recovery or rehab programmes presented as treatment of a specific injury.
- Ember's rehab content is the nearest point to the line. The project rule that it is "general guidance, never diagnosis" matches the MHRA's "tips or advice" and "see your GP" examples. An AI assistant that tells a user what injury they have or what dose of anything to take would cross it; the assistant's instructions should refuse diagnosis and signpost a GP or physiotherapist.
- Wearable heart rate for training load is "sport or fitness". Flagging possible illness from heart-rate or HRV data would be "investigating physiological processes" and should not be offered without advice.
- A BMI or calorie-target calculation for wellness is not decision support for healthcare, but a feature that tells a user they are clinically obese and should follow a treatment plan moves towards a medical purpose.
- Carry the "not a medical device" sentence in the listing (Google requires it) but do not treat it as protection: the MHRA says it does not work if medical claims appear elsewhere.

### Gaps
- Neither the MHRA guidance nor the sources I read on MDCG 2019-11 names calorie counters or diet apps as an example. The conclusion rests on the general fitness and lifestyle wording.
- I did not read MDCG 2019-11 Rev.1 itself, only summaries.
- Whether the UK's planned reform of software-as-a-medical-device regulation has changed the test in 2026 was not checked; the MHRA guidance in force still cites UK MDR 2002.
- A regulatory specialist should review any feature that uses AI to interpret symptoms, injuries or wearable anomalies.

## 7. Eating-disorder and vulnerable-user safeguards

### Takeaway
Studies consistently associate calorie-tracking apps with eating-disorder symptoms, but experiments have not shown that tracking causes them in low-risk users. Beat has called for screening at sign-up, clear signposting and blocking (not just warning) underweight users. Market practice is an 18+ age rule, minimum calorie targets (reported as 1,200 kcal for women and 1,500 for men at MyFitnessPal) and links to eating-disorder help. No UK or Irish regulator rule or coroner's report specific to calorie apps was found.

### Cited Findings
Evidence
- Levinson, Fewell and Brosof (2017), 105 people diagnosed with eating disorders: about 75% had used MyFitnessPal and 73% of those users said it had contributed to their eating disorder. — [PubMed 28843591](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28843591/)
- Simpson and Mazzeo (2017), 493 college students: those who used calorie trackers showed higher eating concern and dietary restraint, controlling for BMI; the authors wrote that "for some individuals, these devices might do more harm than good." — [PubMed 28214452](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28214452/)
- A 2019 JMIR mHealth study of young adults reported that around half of participants had some negative experiences with healthy eating or fitness apps (as summarised in search results; not read in full). — [JMIR mHealth 2019](https://mhealth.jmir.org/2019/6/e14239/)
- Experimental work reported no evidence that using MyFitnessPal causes eating-disorder symptoms in general or low-risk samples, including a one-month trial in low-risk undergraduate women (as summarised in search results; individual papers not read). — [PMC9109125](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9109125); [PMC8485346](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8485346)
- A 2025 systematic review (Wallace et al.) found diet and fitness app use associated with disordered eating, body image concerns and compulsive exercise, with inconsistent conclusions across studies. — [University of Hertfordshire repository](https://uhra.herts.ac.uk/id/eprint/27021/2/wallace-et-al-2025-a-systematic-search-and-review-focusing-on-the-influence-of-health-tracking-technologies-on-eating.pdf)
- A June 2026 news feature reported that nutrition apps' gaming features carry risks for some users (not read in full). — [WTOP/AP, June 2026](https://wtop.com/lifestyle/2026/06/nutrition-apps-can-help-build-healthy-habits-for-some-users-their-gaming-features-carry-risks/)

Charity positions
- Beat's Tom Quinn (reported 2019 to 2023): calorie-tracking apps can be "incredibly attractive" to people with an eating disorder; apps should carry out "stringent health checks, including an eating disorder screening when people first join the app as well as regular check-ins"; weight or BMI alone does not show whether someone has an eating disorder; support must be clearly signposted. — [Tyla, April 2023](https://tyla.com/health/calorie-counting-apps-impact-eating-disorders-354995-20230403); [Refinery29, 2019](https://www.refinery29.com/en-gb/2019/07/235423/meal-tracking-apps-eating-disorder)
- Beat on the NHS weight loss app: telling people with a low BMI that the app was not suitable "went no further in blocking their access", which Beat considered insufficient; it later welcomed changes that deter people with or vulnerable to eating disorders. — [Beat](https://beateatingdisorders.org.uk/news/beat-campaigners-celebrate-improvement-in-nhs-app)
- Beat opposes mandatory calorie labelling on menus because it "risks causing great distress for people suffering from or vulnerable to eating disorders". — [Beat](https://beateatingdisorders.org.uk/news/beats-response-government-plan-calorie-count)

Market practice
- MyFitnessPal terms (effective 18 March 2025): "You must be at least 18 years old to use the Services." "We promote healthy relationships with food and don't condone dangerously low levels of eating." "Any use of our Services to promote, glamorize, or achieve dangerously low levels of eating is not permitted." It disclaims medical advice and links to eating-disorder resources. — [MyFitnessPal Terms](https://www.myfitnesspal.com/terms-of-service)
- MyFitnessPal reportedly will not set a goal below 1,200 kcal a day for women or 1,500 for men, and shows a message when logged intake is too low (press report; not confirmed against MyFitnessPal documentation). It told the press it uses "specific safeguards to reduce its attraction for individuals attempting to use it to enable detrimental eating behaviours". — [Tyla, April 2023](https://tyla.com/health/calorie-counting-apps-impact-eating-disorders-354995-20230403); [MyFitnessPal support: eating disorder resources](https://support.myfitnesspal.com/hc/en-us/articles/360032625071)
- Noom terms: users must be at least 18 ("You cannot use the Service if you are under 18 years old", per a third-party tracker of the April 2026 terms). — [Noom Terms](https://www.noom.com/legal/terms-and-conditions/); [ConductAtlas](https://conductatlas.com/platform/noom/noom-terms-of-service/minimum-age-requirement-of-eighteen/)

Clinical and advertising benchmarks
- NICE NG246 (overweight and obesity management, 2025): dietary approaches should keep energy intake below expenditure; low-energy diets (800 to 1,200 kcal a day) only as part of a multicomponent strategy with long-term support in a specialist service; very-low-energy diets (under 800 kcal) only in specialist settings for specific clinical needs; neither as a long-term strategy; advise people not to use restrictive, nutritionally unbalanced diets. — [NICE NG246: physical activity and diet](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/NG246/chapter/physical-activity-and-diet)
- CAP Code 13.3 uses BMI below 18.5 as the point at which weight reduction is potentially harmful; 13.7 requires medical advice for diets under 800 kcal a day. — [CAP Code section 13](https://www.asa.org.uk/type/non_broadcast/code_section/13.html)
- UK coroners have issued several Prevention of Future Deaths reports on anorexia deaths (for example the 2021 report after five inquests, addressed to the Health Secretary), concerned with clinical training, under-reporting and service gaps. None of those found mentions calorie or fitness apps. — [Averil Hart PFD report](https://www.judiciary.uk/wp-content/uploads/2021/03/Averil-Hart-2021-0058-Redacted.pdf); [Karen Graham PFD report, 2026](https://www.judiciary.uk/prevention-of-future-death-reports/karen-graham-prevention-of-future-deaths-report/)
- UK consumer law treats people as vulnerable "because of age, physical or mental health, credulity or the circumstances they are in" where the trader could reasonably foresee it. — [CMA207](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/unfair-commercial-practices-cma207/unfair-commercial-practices)

### Inferences
Safeguards that the evidence, Beat's statements, NICE and market practice together support (design suggestions, not legal requirements):
- 18+ only, with a neutral date-of-birth question.
- A floor on the daily calorie target. NICE places diets of 800 to 1,200 kcal in specialist services, so a self-serve app should not set a target at or below 1,200; 1,200 for women and 1,500 for men is the reported market convention. Which numbers Ember uses is a clinical judgement worth a dietitian's sign-off.
- A floor on goal weight: do not offer a weight-loss goal to a user whose BMI is under 18.5 or whose goal weight would be under BMI 18.5. Beat's criticism of the NHS app indicates this should be a block (offer maintain or gain instead), not a dismissible warning.
- A cap on goal pace at 1 kg a week, in line with CAP 13.10 and the ASAI rule.
- An optional, respectful screening question at onboarding ("Have you ever had, or are you being treated for, an eating disorder?") with a route that hides calorie numbers or signposts help. Beat asked for screening and check-ins; note the answer is itself health data needing the explicit consent in section 3.
- Signposting in the app and in the low-intake message: Beat (UK) and Bodywhys (Ireland), plus "speak to your GP".
- Gamification: do not reward under-eating. No XP, streak or praise for finishing under target by a wide margin or for larger deficits; reward logging, not restriction. The existing streak break supports this. This is where game mechanics and eating-disorder risk meet, and the 2026 press coverage shows it is being noticed.
- The AI assistant should refuse requests for very low calorie plans, purging or rapid loss, and should signpost.
- A regulator assessing Ember under consumer law would treat users with eating disorders as a foreseeable vulnerable group.

### Gaps
- No Bodywhys statement on calorie-tracking apps was found.
- No current (2024 to 2026) Beat policy statement specifically on calorie-counting apps was found on Beat's own site; the quotes come from press reports of 2019 to 2023.
- MyFitnessPal's 1,200 and 1,500 kcal floors are from a press report; Lose It!'s terms could not be opened, and its App Store listings show conflicting age ratings (4+ and 18+) across regions.
- NICE NG69 (eating disorders) was not retrieved.
- No Apple or Google policy specific to eating-disorder safeguards in diet apps was found.
- No UK or Irish coroner, ICO, DPC, CMA or ASA commentary specific to calorie apps and eating disorders was found.
- Not researched in depth: the UK Online Safety Act 2023. Ember's squads (invite-only boards) and sharing features may make it a regulated user-to-user service with duties to assess illegal-content risk and, if children are likely to use it, risks to children; eating-disorder content is a named concern of that regime. Ofcom's guidance should be checked with a specialist. — [Ofcom: illegal content duties](https://www.ofcom.org.uk/online-safety/illegal-and-harmful-content/illegal-content-duties-under-the-online-safety-act); [Ofcom: children's duties](https://www.ofcom.org.uk/online-safety/illegal-and-harmful-content/quick-guide-to-childrens-safety-codes)

## 8. Accessibility duties that touch onboarding

### Takeaway
The European Accessibility Act has applied in Ireland since 28 June 2025 through S.I. 636/2023, but microenterprises (fewer than 10 people and turnover or balance sheet up to €2 million) that provide services are exempt, so Ember is probably outside it for now. The UK Equality Act duty to make reasonable adjustments does apply to a business serving UK consumers and has no size exemption. WCAG 2.2 AA is the practical benchmark for the sign-up flow.

### Cited Findings
- Ireland transposed the Act by S.I. No. 636 of 2023; it applies from 28 June 2025. — [Irish Statute Book](https://www.irishstatutebook.ie/eli/2023/si/636/made/en/); [gov.ie](https://www.gov.ie/en/department-of-children-disability-and-equality/publications/european-accessibility-act)
- "The requirements and obligations of the European Accessibility Act (EAA) do not apply to microenterprises providing services." A microenterprise "employs fewer than 10 persons" and has "annual turnover not exceeding €2 million" or a balance sheet total not exceeding €2 million. — [CCPC: guidelines for microenterprises](https://www.ccpc.ie/business/enforcement/accessibility/european-accessibility-act-guidelines-for-microenterprises/)
- The CCPC supervises "E-commerce services" and e-books under the Act; the harmonised standard is EN 301 549; enforcement can include "fines and imprisonment in exceptional cases". — [CCPC: guidelines for microenterprises](https://www.ccpc.ie/business/enforcement/accessibility/european-accessibility-act-guidelines-for-microenterprises/)
- UK: the Equality Act 2010 requires service providers, including private businesses, to make reasonable adjustments, and the duty is anticipatory; websites and apps count as services; the Act names no technical standard, and WCAG 2.2 AA is widely used as the benchmark (secondary sources). The Equality and Human Rights Commission enforces. — [Scope for Business](https://business.scope.org.uk/uk-law-and-digital-accessibility-what-it-means-for-your-organisation/); [Law Society](https://lawsociety.org.uk/topics/hr-and-people-management/digital-accessibility); [Taylor Wessing](https://www.taylorwessing.com/de/interface/2022/the-european-accessibility-act/what-is-the-uks-approach-to-accessibility)
- WCAG 2.2 criteria new in 2.2 that bear on sign-up: 3.3.7 Redundant Entry (A), do not make people re-enter "Information previously entered by or provided to the user"; 3.3.8 Accessible Authentication (Minimum) (AA), "A cognitive function test (such as remembering a password or solving a puzzle) is not required for any step" unless an alternative or assistance is available; 2.5.8 Target Size (Minimum) (AA), "at least 24 by 24 CSS pixels"; 2.4.11 Focus Not Obscured (Minimum) (AA); 2.5.7 Dragging Movements (AA), anything done by dragging "can be achieved by a single pointer without dragging"; 3.2.6 Consistent Help (A). — [W3C: What's new in WCAG 2.2](https://www.w3.org/WAI/standards-guidelines/wcag/new-in-22/)

### Inferences
- If Ember's owner operates as a business with fewer than 10 people and under €2 million turnover, the EAA does not bind it today. The exemption ends on growth, and a free app with no consumer contract for payment may also sit outside "e-commerce services"; neither point should be relied on as a reason to skip accessibility, because the UK duty applies anyway.
- Onboarding points to check against WCAG 2.2 AA: every field has a visible, programmatic label (not placeholder only); errors are described in text next to the field and not by colour alone; the password field allows paste and password managers (3.3.8), and the emailed-link option already gives a no-memory route; height, weight and date pickers work without dragging (a slider or wheel needs a typed alternative, 2.5.7); targets are at least 24 px (the project's 320 px check helps); sex and goal choices are real radio buttons that screen readers announce; nothing times out; text contrast holds in light and dark; the floating voice button does not cover a focused field (2.4.11); the consent statement is readable by a screen reader before the button.
- Voice logging is an accessibility benefit, but must not be the only way to do anything.

### Gaps
- Whether a free consumer app is an "e-commerce service" under S.I. 636/2023 was not settled by the sources; I did not read the definition in Regulation 2.
- The EHRC statutory Code was not read; the Equality Act points come from secondary sources.
- Ireland's Equal Status Acts (reasonable accommodation by service providers) were not researched.
- No enforcement example against a small app in Ireland or the UK was found.

## 9. Email and notification marketing consent at sign-up

### Takeaway
Marketing email needs its own opt-in at sign-up in both countries; it cannot be a condition of creating the account or bundled into the terms, and a pre-ticked box does not count. The "soft opt-in" for existing customers depends on a sale or negotiation for a sale, which is doubtful for a free app. Service messages (verification, password reset, security) are not marketing and need no consent.

### Cited Findings
- UK (PECR regulation 22): consent to marketing email "must be freely given. It's unlikely to be valid consent if you make agreeing to marketing a necessary condition for buying your product or service". The soft opt-in under regulation 22(3) has five conditions that must all apply, including that details were obtained in the course of a sale or negotiations for a sale, marketing is for the sender's own similar products, and an opt-out was offered at collection and in every message; it covers commercial marketing only. — [ICO: PECR electronic mail marketing rules](https://ico.org.uk/for-organisations/direct-marketing-and-privacy-and-electronic-communications/guidance-on-direct-marketing-using-electronic-mail/how-do-we-comply-with-the-pecr-electronic-mail-marketing-rules/); [ICO: electronic mail marketing](https://ico.org.uk/for-organisations/direct-marketing-and-privacy-and-electronic-communications/guide-to-pecr/electronic-and-telephone-marketing/electronic-mail-marketing/)
- The ICO updated its electronic mail guidance on 28 April 2026; the only new soft opt-in is for charities (regulation 22(3A), from 5 February 2026). Maximum PECR fines are now £17.5 million or 4% of global turnover. — [Lewis Silkin](https://www.lewissilkin.com/insights/2026/05/08/the-charitable-purposes-soft-opt-in-ico-issues-updated-direct-marketing-guidanc-102ms57); [DLA Piper](https://privacymatters.dlapiper.com/2026/02/uk-commencement-of-the-data-protection-provisions-in-the-data-use-and-access-act/)
- Ireland (Regulation 13 of S.I. 336/2011): electronic direct marketing generally needs prior opt-in consent. The existing-customer exception (Regulation 13(11)) requires that the details were obtained in the context of a sale, the product marketed is similar, an opt-out was offered at collection and in each message, and the first marketing message is sent within 12 months of the sale. — [DPC: FAQ on consent for electronic direct marketing](https://www.dataprotection.ie/en/dpc-guidance/faq-consent-electronic-direct-marketing); [DPC: direct marketing](https://www.dataprotection.ie/dpc-guidance/blogs/direct-marketing-what-you-need-know-about-direct-marketing)
- Consent standard is the GDPR one: not bundled with terms, no pre-ticked boxes, granular. — [ICO: What is valid consent?](https://ico.org.uk/for-organisations/uk-gdpr-guidance-and-resources/lawful-basis/consent/what-is-valid-consent/)
- Apple: an app "may not require users to enable system functionalities (such as push notifications ...)" to use it. — [Apple App Review Guidelines 5.1.2(i)](https://developer.apple.com/app-store/review/guidelines/)
- CMA banned practice 28: "Making persistent and unwanted solicitations by any means". — [CMA207](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/unfair-commercial-practices-cma207/unfair-commercial-practices)

### Inferences
- At account creation: no marketing consent inside "I agree to the terms". If Ember wants to email news or offers, add one unticked, optional line ("Email me product news and tips") separate from the health-data consent, with unsubscribe in every email and a setting in the app.
- Because Ember is free, there is no "sale" on which to hang the soft opt-in in Ireland, and the UK position ("sale or negotiations for a sale") is at best arguable for a free sign-up. Use opt-in consent for all marketing email in both countries.
- Emails that are marketing even if they feel like service: "we miss you" win-back emails, feature promotion, upgrade offers, referral prompts. Emails that are service: sign-in link, password reset, security alerts, a coach-link request the user is party to, legally required notices. A weekly review email sits in between: treat it as opt-in.
- Push notifications: the OS permission is separate from legal consent. Meal and streak reminders the user switches on are a requested feature. Promotional pushes should have their own opt-in, and ask for the OS permission at the moment the user turns on reminders, not during onboarding.
- Do not use health data (weight, goal, diary) to target marketing without separate explicit consent; Apple 5.1.3 bars HealthKit-derived data from marketing outright.

### Gaps
- Neither regulator's guidance, as retrieved, addresses a free-app sign-up directly; whether a free account is "negotiations for a sale" in the UK is unsettled. Opt-in avoids the question.
- Whether PECR or the Irish Regulations treat push notifications as "electronic mail" was not confirmed from the regulators' text in this session.
- The DPC FAQ is dated April 2020 (re-uploaded 2026); check for a newer version.
- The EU ePrivacy rules on cookies and similar storage (relevant to the web app's `localStorage` use and any analytics) were not researched; the project already bars analytics without the owner's say.
